\section{Summary of Contributions}
\label{sec:disc:contributions}
% ============================================================================

This thesis makes the following contributions, organised by the three computational artefacts and the integrative system:

\textbf{Contribution 1: FuzzyArcLoss V2 (Artefact 1).}
A novel angular margin loss function that introduces per-sample fuzzy membership modulation for histopathological pattern classification.
Unlike fixed-margin losses (ArcFace, CosFace, SphereFace) that apply the same penalty to all training samples, FuzzyArcLoss~V2 adapts the margin strength based on classification confidence through an explicit fuzzy membership function (Equation~\ref{eq:membership_v2}).
The method achieves 92.31\% macro-F1 on six LUAD histological patterns (15 runs, $p = 0.001$ vs SphereFace), with particular improvement on the minority cribriform class (83.6\% F1).
The systematic identification and correction of 20 pipeline issues---contributing $+$18 percentage points to performance across all loss functions---provides practical guidance for the application of angular margin losses to small histopathological datasets.

\textbf{Contribution 2: Pattern-Informed ABMIL (Artefact 2).}
A multiple instance learning architecture that fuses CTransPath visual embeddings with fuzzy pattern membership probabilities, injecting validated histological domain knowledge as a structured prior.
The method achieves mutation prediction AUROCs competitive with embedding-only ABMIL (mean AUROC 0.658) while enabling a six-level interpretability framework: spatial attention maps reveal \emph{where} the model attends, pattern attribution reveals \emph{what} it sees, SHAP decomposition quantifies embedding versus pattern contributions, per-slide feature channel ablation reveals whether patterns \emph{actually help or harm} each prediction, and fuzzy linguistic labels classify all numeric XAI outputs into calibrated intensity terms injected into LLM explanations.
This combination allows a pathologist to verify and critique the model's reasoning against the original slide, addressing the interpretability gap in existing mutation prediction methods.

\textbf{Contribution 3: Fuzzy Choquet MIL (Artefact 3).}
An aggregation mechanism that augments standard attention pooling with a
parallel Choquet integral branch operating on tile-level fuzzy pattern
memberships under a learned 2-additive fuzzy measure.
A general fuzzy measure over $n$ sources requires $2^n - 2$ parameters
($n = 6 \Rightarrow 62$), which is both computationally expensive and
prone to overfitting on small datasets.
The 2-additive restriction limits interactions to pairs, reducing the
parameter count to $n + \binom{n}{2} = 6 + 15 = 21$ while still
capturing the most informative pairwise synergies (e.g., solid +
micropapillary $\to$ TP53) and redundancies.
This interpretable component---21 learnable parameters (6 Shapley
values $+$ 15 interaction indices)---operates within a hybrid
architecture of $\sim$266{,}000
total parameters, achieving mean AUROC 0.653---competitive with the
$\sim$200{,}000-parameter ABMIL baseline (0.658)---and is the sole top
performer for KRAS (0.609) and RBM10 (0.661).
The Shapley values reveal learned pattern--gene associations
(cribriform and solid receive the highest singleton importance for
KRAS; near-uniform values for KEAP1 confirm no dominant pattern),
and the interaction indices reveal synergistic pattern
co-occurrences (lepidic$\times$solid for KRAS) that standard
attention mechanisms cannot model.
All numeric Choquet outputs are classified by fuzzy linguistic labels
(e.g., ``Near-Uniform'' profile, ``Moderate Synergy'') that are
injected into LLM prompts to prevent hallucination of intensity terms.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first application of the
Choquet integral as a \emph{learned aggregation operator over
domain-specific semantic memberships} in computational pathology.
Prior work has used the Choquet integral exclusively as an ensemble
fusion operator---combining outputs of multiple CNN classifiers with
fuzzy measures computed heuristically from classifier accuracy or
coalition game theory~\cite{bhowal2021fuzzybach}.
The closest methodological precedent, the Multiple Instance Choquet
Integral (MICI) of McCurley and Zare~\cite{mccurley2023mici}, operates
within a MIL framework but for remote sensing classifier fusion rather
than for semantic feature aggregation over histological patterns.
The present work differs in three respects: the Choquet inputs are
fuzzy pattern membership degrees (not classifier scores), the
2-additive fuzzy measure is learned end-to-end via backpropagation
(not pre-computed), and the resulting Shapley values and interaction
indices yield biologically interpretable mutation--morphology
associations rather than purely technical model diagnostics.

\textbf{Contribution 4: LCHAI Clinical Decision Support System.}
An integrative prototype that demonstrates the translational potential of the three artefacts within a unified clinical workflow.
LCHAI combines tile-level pattern classification overlay, slide-level mutation prediction with confidence calibration (Conclusive/Inconclusive labelling), a six-level interpretability framework (attention maps, pattern overlay, SHAP decomposition, Choquet Shapley values, per-slide feature channel ablation, and fuzzy linguistic labels), a biomedical knowledge graph linking mutations to therapeutic targets, and a dual anti-hallucination mechanism (KG grounding + fuzzy label injection) for LLM-generated explanations.

\textbf{Contribution 5: Analytical Insight --- Gene-Dependent Utility of Fuzzy Representations.}
The finding that the relative benefit of fuzzy versus crisp pattern
representations is gene-dependent constitutes an original analytical
contribution.
Soft (fuzzy) probabilities provide the clearest advantage for genes
with diffuse or heterogeneous morphological correlates---KEAP1
($+$0.016 AUROC) and RBM10 ($+$0.017)---where uncertainty across
pattern boundaries carries diagnostic information that hard labels
discard.
For genes with well-defined morphological correlates (TP53, EGFR,
KRAS, STK11), fuzzy and crisp representations perform equivalently
(differences $\leq$0.004, within fold variance).
This result connects the biological nature of the
mutation--morphology relationship to the appropriate representation
formalism, offering guidance for future work on pattern-informed
mutation prediction.
The per-slide feature channel ablation
(Section~\ref{sec:meth:interp:ablation},
Figures~\ref{fig:attn_tp53_tp}--\ref{fig:attn_rbm10_tp})
reinforces this finding at the individual case level: KRAS is the
only representative case where adding patterns measurably improves
the prediction ($+7.6$\,pp), while for TP53 ($-16.1$\,pp) and
KEAP1 ($-44.7$\,pp) patterns introduce interference---confirming
that the utility of pattern knowledge is not merely gene-dependent
at the population level but also slide-dependent at the individual
level.

\textbf{Contribution 6: Ontology-Grounded Explanation Layer.}
A three-tier knowledge graph architecture that links predicted
histological patterns and mutation hypotheses to standardised biomedical
knowledge without influencing model training or prediction.
The curated layer encodes established clinical evidence from WHO~2021,
OncoKB/FDA, TCGA/CIViC, and COSMIC as IRI-grounded assertions with
explicit provenance.
The dynamic layer filters the graph per case to retain only
morphologically relevant entities.
The DeepSearch layer continuously mines PubMed, Semantic Scholar, and
arXiv for new pattern--mutation--treatment associations, validating
them against type-constraint rules and merging them as versioned
additions.
This architecture provides ontology-grounded, auditable explanations
(RQ6) and continuous knowledge enrichment (RQ7)---capabilities absent
from all prior H\&E-based mutation prediction methods.

\section{Interpretation of Results}
\label{sec:disc:interpretation}
% ============================================================================
\subsection{On the Role of Domain Knowledge Under Data Scarcity}
\label{sec:disc:interp:domain}

The central premise of this thesis is that incorporating validated
domain knowledge---specifically, expert-defined histological patterns
via fuzzy logic---can compensate for limited training data.
The empirical results support this premise partially, and the nature
of that partial support is itself informative.

On one hand, the Fuzzy Choquet MIL achieves competitive mutation
prediction with a fuzzy measure of only 21 interpretable parameters
operating on 6 pattern features in a parallel branch alongside a
shared embedding encoder ($\sim$266{,}000 total model parameters),
matching or exceeding ABMIL ($\sim$200{,}000 parameters) on
512-dimensional embeddings for two of six genes (KRAS and RBM10).
The interpretability of the fuzzy measure is possible precisely because
the domain knowledge---pattern definitions trained on the independent
Zenodo ANORAK dataset---provides a strong inductive bias that
eliminates the need to discover morphological structure from mutation
labels alone.
The model is not asked to learn that solid morphology exists; it is
told this directly through the pattern probabilities, and must only
learn how the distribution of known patterns aggregates to predict
each gene.

On the other hand, the pattern-informed ABMIL (P) does not achieve
a clear AUROC improvement over the embedding-only baseline (B2) at
the global level (both 0.658).
The CTransPath embeddings, pretrained on 15 million histopathology
tiles, already encode morphological information that subsumes the
six-pattern taxonomy.
At the aggregate level, the pattern channel is therefore marginally
redundant for prediction purposes.
Gene-level SHAP decompositions show that patterns receive
substantial attribution (39\% for TP53, 41\% for KRAS, 60\% for
KEAP1), but the per-slide feature channel ablation
(Section~\ref{sec:meth:interp:ablation}) reveals that high SHAP
attribution does not imply positive contribution:
patterns measurably improve prediction only for KRAS ($+7.6$\,pp),
while actively degrading it for TP53 ($-16.1$\,pp) and
KEAP1 ($-44.7$\,pp).
Crucially, the pattern channel remains essential for
\emph{interpretability}, where it provides the critical link between
attention weights and named histological categories---even when
patterns hurt prediction, their presence enables the system to
\emph{explain why} they hurt.

This observation points to a nuanced and practically important
conclusion: \emph{domain knowledge helps most when the downstream
model lacks the capacity or data to learn the relevant features
independently.}
Within the Fuzzy Choquet MIL, the 21-parameter fuzzy measure branch
benefits substantially from the explicit pattern prior because it
operates exclusively on 6-d pattern features---a space where domain
knowledge is the \emph{only} source of information.
The shared embedding pathway (present in both ABMIL and FC), with its
access to 512-dimensional pretrained features, can approximate much
of the same morphological information from raw features alone.
For settings with even fewer slides ($< 200$), or with weaker
pretrained encoders, the advantage of domain knowledge injection
would likely be more pronounced---a hypothesis that post-thesis
studies with smaller institutional cohorts could test directly.


\subsection{On the Interpretability--Performance Trade-Off}
\label{sec:disc:interp:tradeoff}

The field of H\&E-based mutation prediction has pursued a trajectory
of increasing scale: from Coudray's 382 slides (2018) to DeepGEM's
3{,}637 patients (2025) and CHIEF's 60{,}530 slides (2024).
Each increase in scale has been accompanied by an increase in model
complexity and a decrease in interpretability.
Foundation models such as CHIEF and GigaPath achieve superior AUROCs
but provide no mechanism for a pathologist to verify the biological
plausibility of individual predictions.

This thesis charts an alternative course.
By enriching the embedding representation with six clinically
validated histological patterns, the approach adds an interpretable
prediction pathway that a pathologist can inspect and validate
\emph{without sacrificing discriminative power}---the pattern-informed
ABMIL (condition~P) matches the embedding-only baseline (B2) in mean
AUROC (both 0.658).
When the model predicts TP53 mutation with probability 0.951
(slide TCGA-99-8025), the pathologist can inspect the attended region
and observe that the top-200 tiles are enriched in
cribriform and solid morphology relative to the slide's 91.5\% micropapillary
background---a biologically plausible signal (micropapillary and solid
are high-grade features associated with TP53 in the
literature~\cite{leighl2002tp53morphology}) that
constitutes a falsifiable hypothesis for pathological review.
This capability is absent from all black-box methods, regardless of
their AUROC.

The trade-off is quantifiable: at comparable data scale ($\sim$700
slides), the pattern-informed approach achieves AUROCs of 0.694--0.717
for clinically actionable genes (EGFR, TP53), compared to the 0.826
reported by Coudray for EGFR on 382 slides.
The $\sim$0.12 AUROC gap is the ``cost of interpretability.''
Whether this cost is acceptable depends on the clinical context.
In a triage setting, where the goal is to prioritise patients for
expedited molecular testing, a moderately accurate but explainable
prediction may be more useful than a highly accurate but opaque one,
because the pathologist can adjust their confidence based on the
explanation.

A third dimension of this trade-off concerns data scalability.
The performance gap between this thesis and large-scale proprietary
studies (DeepGEM: 0.82--0.96 on 3{,}637 patients; EAGLE: 0.890 on
8{,}461 slides) reflects a structural data asymmetry rather than a
methodological ceiling.
Two lines of evidence support this interpretation.

First, within the public-data regime, this thesis achieves competitive
or superior results: Pattern-Informed ABMIL surpasses
Saldanha~et~al.~\cite{saldanha2023npj} ($\sim$0.65 for TP53) and
Logan~et~al.~\cite{logan2022medrxiv} (0.692 for TP53) at matched data
scale ($N \approx 700$ slides), suggesting that the fuzzy
pattern-informed approach extracts more information per slide than
prior methods operating in the same data regime.

Second, the Fuzzy Choquet MIL achieves competitive mutation prediction
with a 21-parameter fuzzy measure on 6-dimensional pattern inputs
(within a hybrid architecture of $\sim$266{,}000 total parameters).
This structured interpretability implies that the approach would
\emph{scale favourably} with additional data: the inductive bias
provided by validated histological patterns reduces the amount of data
required to learn mutation-relevant features, potentially narrowing
the performance gap with foundation models at intermediate data scales
($N \approx 2{,}000$--$5{,}000$ slides).
Whether the approach would match or exceed large-scale proprietary
methods at full data scale remains an open empirical question---one
that a prospective multi-centre study could address as a direct
extension of this work (Section~\ref{sec:disc:future:long}).

It is important to note that the comparison with large-scale studies
is not the primary frame of evaluation for this thesis.
In the Design Science Research framework, artefacts are evaluated
against their stated design objectives---interpretability, robustness
to data scarcity, and principled integration of domain knowledge---
not against absolute AUROC rankings.
By these criteria, all three artefacts meet or partially meet their
success criteria (Section~\ref{sec:eval:rq}), and the LCHAI~v2.0
prototype demonstrates a clinical utility pathway that no prior
mutation prediction system offers.

Three additional dimensions of this comparison merit explicit
discussion, as they constitute structural advantages of the proposed
approach that AUROC alone cannot capture:

\paragraph{Multi-level interpretability versus black-box prediction.}
DeepGEM, EAGLE, CHIEF and GigaPath provide a single output: a mutation
probability score.
LCHAI~v2.0 simultaneously provides six layers of interpretability for
every prediction: (i)~a spatial pattern overlay, (ii)~an ABMIL attention
map, (iii)~a SHAP decomposition quantifying embedding versus pattern
contributions, (iv)~Choquet Shapley values and interaction indices,
(v)~per-slide feature channel ablation comparing three independently
trained models, and (vi)~fuzzy linguistic labels that classify all
numeric XAI outputs into calibrated intensity terms (e.g., ``Moderate
Synergy,'' ``Embedding-Led'') injected into LLM-generated explanations
to prevent hallucination.
This multi-level interpretability enables a pathologist to verify,
critique, and contextualise each prediction---a prerequisite for
clinical adoption that no competing method satisfies.

\paragraph{Gene-dependent optimal method selection.}
A novel analytical finding of this thesis is that the relative utility
of fuzzy pattern representations is \emph{gene-dependent}: embedding-only
ABMIL (B2) is optimal for TP53 and EGFR, whose morphological correlates
manifest as sub-cellular texture captured by CTransPath; the Fuzzy
Choquet MIL (FC) is optimal for KRAS and RBM10, where inter-pattern
interactions carry the discriminative signal; and the proposed concat
(P) is optimal for STK11 and KEAP1, whose diffuse multi-pattern
correlates benefit from explicit pattern injection alongside embeddings.
DeepGEM applies a single architecture uniformly to all genes, forgoing
this biologically grounded specificity.
LCHAI~v2.0 implements gene-dependent method selection based on this
finding, using the optimal model per gene as determined by the K-fold
benchmark---a design principle absent from all competing systems.

\paragraph{Intrinsic interpretability and reproducibility.}
The Fuzzy Choquet MIL achieves mean AUROC 0.653 across six genes
using a fuzzy measure with only 21 interpretable parameters
(6 Shapley values $+$ 15 interaction indices) within a hybrid
architecture of $\sim$266{,}000 total parameters---comparable in
scale to ABMIL ($\sim$200{,}000) yet offering intrinsic
Shapley-value interpretability that black-box attention lacks.
By contrast, DeepGEM's co-supervised MIL contains millions of
parameters with no comparable interpretive structure.
The interpretable fuzzy measure demonstrates that validated domain
knowledge---the six histological patterns, encoded as fuzzy
memberships---provides an inductive bias strong enough to concentrate
mutation-relevant signal into a transparent, low-dimensional
aggregation component.
Furthermore, all results in this thesis are fully reproducible from
public data (TCGA-LUAD, 687 slides) with publicly available code,
whereas DeepGEM's results depend on proprietary clinical data from
16 Chinese hospitals that no external group can access or verify.


\subsection{On the Genotype--Phenotype Mismatch}
\label{sec:disc:genotype_phenotype}

A recurrent finding throughout the evaluation is that mutation
prediction from histology produces \emph{false negatives}: cases
where the MAF ground truth confirms a mutation, yet the model
assigns a low probability because the tissue morphology does not
match the population-level association learned during training.
The EGFR case TCGA-86-8280 (Section~\ref{sec:eval:interp:attention})
is a clear example: a confirmed EGFR mutation
receives P(mut)~$= 0.076$ despite the slide being 94.9\%
micropapillary with very little of the canonical EGFR-associated
patterns (lepidic 0.3\%, papillary 0.05\%).
This false negative suggests that when canonical patterns are absent,
the model cannot leverage the typical
well-differentiated morphology associated with canonical EGFR
mutations (exon~19 deletions, L858R).

This phenomenon reflects a fundamental biological reality:
\textbf{the genotype--phenotype relationship in cancer is
statistical, not deterministic.}
Three distinct mechanisms contribute to the mismatch:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Incomplete morphological penetrance.}
    Not every mutation produces a visible change in tissue
    architecture.
    A missense mutation may alter protein function without
    reorganising cellular growth patterns, leaving the H\&E
    appearance indistinguishable from wild-type tissue.
    This is particularly relevant for mutations at non-canonical
    sites within a gene (e.g., an in-frame deletion versus the
    classic exon~19 deletion for EGFR).

    \item \textbf{Morphological convergence.}
    Different mutations can produce similar tissue phenotypes.
    Solid growth pattern, for example, is associated with
    TP53~\cite{leighl2002tp53morphology}
    \emph{and} KRAS~\cite{shim2015krasmucinous} \emph{and} poor
    differentiation regardless of driver gene.
    When multiple genes share a morphological correlate, the model
    cannot unambiguously attribute the observed pattern to a single
    mutation.

    \item \textbf{Intra-tumoral heterogeneity.}
    A single tumour slide may contain a mosaic of subclones with
    different mutation profiles, only one of which carries the
    target mutation.
    The mutation label in the MAF file applies to the
    \emph{sequenced region} (typically a macrodissected fragment),
    which may not correspond to the morphologically dominant region
    in the WSI.
\end{enumerate}

A fourth mechanism, revealed by the MAF analysis of the representative
slides (Table~\ref{tab:enrichment_summary}), is
\textbf{co-mutation interference}.
Five of six representative slides carry two or more simultaneous driver
mutations (e.g., TCGA-55-7815: KRAS + KEAP1 + RBM10;
TCGA-49-AAR0: TP53 + STK11 + KEAP1).
The tissue morphology of a co-mutated tumour is a superposition of the
phenotypic effects of all its driver mutations.
When the model is asked to predict a single gene (e.g., STK11), it
observes a morphological signal that is a mixture of all co-occurring
mutations---none of which can be cleanly attributed to the target gene.
Since each gene model is trained with independent binary labels, it
cannot exploit co-mutation patterns (e.g., the clinically recognised
KRAS--STK11 co-occurrence that produces a distinctive immune-cold
phenotype).
This limitation motivates a multi-task prediction architecture as
future work (Section~\ref{sec:disc:future:long}).

Together, these four mechanisms---incomplete penetrance, morphological
convergence, intra-tumoral heterogeneity, and co-mutation
interference---explain why the best-reported AUROCs for single-gene
mutation prediction from H\&E histology plateau around 0.80--0.85
even with proprietary datasets of thousands of
slides~\cite{coudray2018classification}.
The remaining 15--20\% error rate reflects an irreducible gap between
what DNA sequences reveal and what tissue architecture shows.

For this thesis, the genotype--phenotype mismatch has three important
implications.
First, it justifies the \emph{Conclusive / Inconclusive} labelling
framework: genes whose cross-validation AUROC falls below 0.70
are honestly flagged as unreliable, directing clinicians to
molecular testing rather than trusting the morphological prediction.
Second, it validates the multi-layered interpretability approach:
when a false negative occurs (e.g., EGFR on TCGA-86-8280), the
pattern overlay, attention map, and SHAP decomposition make the
\emph{reason} for the failure transparent---the clinician can see
that competing patterns overwhelmed the EGFR signal, and can
decide to order molecular testing on that basis.
This ``transparent failure'' mode, where the system explains why it
is uncertain, is a stronger clinical contribution than a black-box
model that simply outputs a wrong probability without explanation.
Third, the prevalence of co-mutations in the representative slides
(5 of 6 carry $\geq 2$ simultaneous driver mutations) suggests that
a \emph{multi-task} prediction architecture---jointly predicting all
six genes and explicitly modelling co-mutation patterns---could
improve sensitivity for genes whose morphological signal is
confounded by co-occurring mutations.
The current per-gene independent models cannot exploit the
information that, for example, KRAS and STK11 are frequently
co-mutated and produce a distinctive immune-cold microenvironment;
a multi-task model could learn this joint phenotype directly.


\subsection{On the Relationship Between Fuzzy Logic and Biological Gradience}
\label{sec:disc:interp:fuzzy}

A recurring theme throughout this thesis is the alignment between fuzzy set theory and the inherent gradience of biological tissue.
Histological patterns do not have crisp boundaries: a tissue region may simultaneously exhibit features of both acinar and papillary growth, with the proportion depending on the plane of sectioning, the degree of tumour differentiation, and the pathologist's interpretation.
The standard approach in computational pathology is to force a discrete assignment (argmax classification), discarding this biological uncertainty.

FuzzyArcLoss~V2 explicitly models this gradience at two levels.
At the training level, the fuzzy membership function reduces the angular margin for samples at pattern boundaries, preventing the model from overfitting to the assigned discrete label.
At the output level, the soft probability vector $\mathbf{p}_i \in \Delta^5$ preserves the inter-pattern uncertainty, which is then propagated to downstream models (ABMIL, Choquet).

The Fuzzy Choquet integral extends this principle to the aggregation level: the 2-additive fuzzy measure models interactions between patterns that arise from the biological co-occurrence of growth patterns within a single tumour.
For KRAS, the positive interaction index between lepidic and solid patterns ($I = +0.032$) suggests the model has learned that the co-presence of these minority patterns within a micropapillary-dominant slide is more predictive than either pattern alone, complementing the singleton Shapley contributions.
Although the magnitude appears small, interaction indices operate under $L_1$ regularisation ($\lambda_I = 0.01$) that penalises large values to prevent overfitting on the limited training set; the surviving non-zero value represents a genuine learned structure.
Crucially, $I = +0.032$ does not translate to a 3.2 percentage-point change in $P(\text{mut})$: the interaction index modifies the fuzzy measure, which shapes the Choquet aggregation of pattern scores, which then passes through a classification head to produce the mutation probability.
The relationship is indirect but mechanistically interpretable: higher measure for the lepidic$\cup$solid coalition means these patterns receive proportionally more influence when both are present on the slide.

A fourth level of fuzzy logic extends this coherence to the
\emph{interpretation} layer: trapezoidal membership functions classify
numeric XAI outputs into linguistic labels such as ``Moderate Synergy''
or ``Embedding-Led'' (Section~\ref{sec:meth:interp:fuzzy_labels}).
These labels are injected verbatim into LLM prompts, creating a dual
anti-hallucination mechanism: the KG constrains \emph{what} the LLM
says, and the fuzzy labels constrain \emph{how} it says it.

This coherent application of fuzzy logic across four levels of the pipeline---training (FuzzyArcLoss), representation (soft probabilities), aggregation (Choquet integral), and interpretation (fuzzy linguistic labels)---positions fuzzy logic as a central rather than peripheral methodological element of the thesis.

\subsection{On the Design Science Research Framing}
\label{sec:disc:interp:dsr}

The Design Science Research methodology proved particularly suitable for this work, which develops computational artefacts to solve a specific real-world problem rather than testing general scientific hypotheses.
The three artefacts correspond to the ``design and development'' activity of the DSR cycle, while the six-condition ablation study corresponds to the ``evaluation'' activity.
The LCHAI system provides the ``demonstration'' activity, showing how the individual artefacts compose into a functional system.

The DSR framing also provides a natural way to discuss the results without claiming a breakthrough in mutation prediction accuracy.
In DSR, an artefact is evaluated against its stated design objectives---not against the state of the art in an absolute sense.
The design objectives of this thesis include interpretability, robustness to data scarcity, and principled integration of domain knowledge, none of which are captured by AUROC alone.
The Fuzzy Choquet MIL, for example, would be considered a failure if evaluated purely on AUROC against DeepGEM (0.653 vs 0.82--0.96), but a success when evaluated on its ability to provide interpretable mutation predictions with built-in Shapley values from only 687 public slides.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:disc:limitations}
% ============================================================================

This section discusses the limitations of this work.

\subsection{Data Limitations}
\label{sec:disc:lim:data}

\textbf{Single-cohort evaluation.}
All primary results are based on the TCGA-LUAD cohort, a dataset collected
from multiple US institutions between 2006 and 2013.
The absence of external validation on an independent cohort (CPTAC-3,
APOLLO, or a non-US hospital) limits the generalisability claims.
Scanner variability, tissue preparation protocols, and population
demographics may differ across institutions, and the models' robustness
to these domain shifts has not been tested.

\textbf{Limited sample size.}
With 687 slides (668 unique patients), the fold-to-fold variance in AUROC
is substantial (std = 0.04--0.10), reducing the statistical power to
detect small differences between conditions.
Several comparisons that appear directionally meaningful
(e.g., P vs B2 on STK11: $+0.011$) do not reach statistical significance.
Larger cohorts would be needed to confirm or reject these trends.

\textbf{Preliminary pathologist validation and no spatial mutation ground truth.}
A board-certified pathologist from SOLCA Ecuador reviewed eight
subregions across five TCGA slides
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:pattern:confusion}), providing the first
external validation of the pattern classifier on inference data.
This preliminary review revealed systematic micropapillary--acinar
disagreement attributable to domain shift
(Section~\ref{sec:disc:lim:data}).
However, the interpretability claims for mutation prediction
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:interpretability}) remain based on alignment
with published clinicopathological associations rather than on a
formal multi-rater concordance study.
A larger study with 2--3 pathologists would provide stronger
evidence for clinical utility.
Moreover, the MAF mutation labels are \emph{patient-level} binary
indicators derived from bulk DNA sequencing
(Section~\ref{sec:uc:tcga:maf}); they do not provide spatial information
about where in the tissue the mutation's phenotypic effect is visible.
The ABMIL attention maps therefore represent the model's hypothesis
about mutation-relevant regions, not a confirmed spatial correspondence.
Emerging spatial transcriptomics technologies (e.g., Visium, MERFISH)
could in principle provide the tile-level mutation ground truth needed
to validate attention maps, but such data is not yet available for the
TCGA-LUAD cohort.

\textbf{Pattern class verification and domain shift.}
The six-class pattern taxonomy used in this thesis follows the
ANORAK annotation scheme (lepidic, acinar, papillary, micropapillary,
solid, cribriform), aligned with the official Zenodo documentation
(\url{https://zenodo.org/records/10016027}).
On the ANORAK validation set, the confusion matrix
(Figure~\ref{fig:confusion_matrix}) shows that acinar, solid, and
lepidic achieve 100\% recall, while cribriform (63\%) and
micropapillary (77\%) are the most confused classes---consistent with
known inter-observer variability~\cite{anorak2024}.
However, a preliminary expert review of five TCGA slides revealed
systematic disagreement: the expert identified acinar morphology in
subregions where the model predicted micropapillary
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:pattern:confusion}).
This domain shift between the ANORAK training cohort (TRACERx, UK)
and the TCGA inference cohort (US) is a known limitation of
histopathological AI models and underscores the need for
multi-cohort validation before clinical deployment.
The aggregate AUROC results (which depend on the 512-d CTransPath
embeddings) are unaffected by this domain shift, but the clinical
interpretability of pattern attributions and Choquet Shapley values
should be interpreted with this caveat.

\subsection{Methodological Limitations}
\label{sec:disc:lim:method}

\textbf{Cribriform class performance.}
The cribriform pattern, with only $\sim$55 training samples, achieves 83.6\% F1---the lowest of all six classes.
Cribriform is a high-grade acinar variant whose accurate identification is clinically important for tumour grading.
The limited training data for this class constrains the overall reliability of the pattern classifier in cribriform-rich tumours.

\textbf{Threshold sensitivity.}
AUROC evaluates ranking ability, but clinical deployment requires a
probability threshold for binary classification.
The representative-slide analysis in
Section~\ref{sec:eval:interp:attention} shows that four of six
mutant slides receive $P(\text{mut}) < 0.10$, far below the default
threshold of 0.5, suggesting that the models learn to \emph{rank}
mutant versus wild-type slides but are not well
\emph{calibrated}---the raw probability should not be interpreted as
a true posterior.
Threshold optimisation (e.g., Youden's $J$ index) or probability
calibration (e.g., Platt scaling) would be required for any clinical
application, and the calibration properties of the models have not
been systematically studied in this thesis.

\textbf{No multi-task learning.}
Each gene is predicted by a separate model, ignoring potential correlations between mutations (e.g., KRAS and STK11 co-mutation is common in LUAD and produces a distinctive immune phenotype).
A multi-task learning architecture that jointly predicts all six genes could leverage these correlations.

\textbf{Pattern taxonomy fixed at six classes.}
The six-class pattern taxonomy (lepidic, acinar, papillary, micropapillary,
solid, cribriform) captures the major ANORAK-annotated growth patterns but may not
include all mutation-relevant morphological features.
Tumour budding (small cell clusters at the invasive edge),
tumour--stroma interface features, and the degree of
tumour-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) density are known to carry
prognostic and predictive information but are not represented in the
current pattern taxonomy.
In particular, the absence of a ``normal tissue'' or ``stroma'' class means
that the classifier cannot directly encode the immune microenvironment
status---a feature relevant to \textit{STK11} prediction, where the ``cold''
tumour phenotype manifests as reduced TILs in stromal regions rather than
as a specific neoplastic pattern.
\subsection{Technical Limitations}
\label{sec:disc:lim:technical}

\textbf{Non-deterministic training.}
Despite setting random seeds, the use of multi-GPU \texttt{DataParallel}, CUDA atomic operations, and GPU-accelerated augmentations introduces non-deterministic variation of approximately $\pm 0.8$ pp in single-run macro-F1.
The 15-run evaluation protocol mitigates this for the pattern classifier, but the mutation prediction benchmark uses a single seed per fold, making it more susceptible to stochastic variation.

\textbf{Embedding dimensionality.}
The 512-dimensional CTransPath embeddings were chosen for consistency with the pattern classifier's projection head.
Higher-dimensional embeddings (768 raw CTransPath, 1024 CONCH, 2048 RetCCL) might capture finer morphological nuances.
A systematic study of embedding dimensionality was not conducted.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:disc:future}
% ============================================================================

The limitations identified above directly suggest several directions for future work, organised by time horizon.

\subsection{Short-Term Extensions (Post-Thesis)}
\label{sec:disc:future:short}

\textbf{External validation.}
The immediate priority is to evaluate the trained models on the three
external cohorts identified in Section~\ref{sec:uc:external}:
CPTAC-3 ($\sim$111 patients), APOLLO ($\sim$87), and EAGLE ($\sim$31).
External validation would transform the thesis results from single-cohort
findings to multi-cohort evidence, substantially strengthening the
generalisability claims.

\textbf{Extended pathologist concordance study.}
The preliminary review by a SOLCA pathologist
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:pattern:confusion}) established a baseline:
one expert evaluated eight subregions across five slides, revealing
domain shift and micropapillary--acinar confusion.
A natural extension is a formal multi-rater study in which 2--3
board-certified pathologists independently evaluate the LCHAI system
on 50 representative slides, measuring: (a)~inter-rater agreement
between pathologists and the pattern classifier, (b)~pathologist
confidence in the mutation predictions with and without the
six-level interpretability overlay, and (c)~time-to-decision for
mutation triage.
This study is the strongest candidate for a post-thesis publication.

\textbf{Threshold calibration.}
Applying Platt scaling or temperature scaling to the mutation
prediction outputs, using a held-out calibration set, would improve
the probability estimates and enable clinically meaningful binary
classification at optimised thresholds.

\textbf{Strategy for high-probability Inconclusive genes.}
A notable finding is the paradox of genes with high individual-case
prediction probability but low cross-validation AUROC---most strikingly
KRAS, which achieves P(mut)~$= 0.965$ on TCGA-55-7815 yet is labelled
\emph{Inconclusive} because the fold-level AUROC (0.609) falls below
the 0.70 threshold.
This paradox arises because AUROC measures \emph{population-level}
ranking ability across all 687 slides, while the per-slide probability
reflects the model's confidence on a \emph{single} case.
A model can produce a very high probability for an individual slide
whose morphological profile strongly matches the learned pattern
(micropapillary-dominant with solid interactions) while still
performing poorly on the broader population where KRAS-associated
morphology is heterogeneous (solid, IMA variants not represented in
the 6-class taxonomy).

Three strategies could address this gap:
(i)~\textbf{Larger and more diverse cohorts:} KRAS has a 20.4\%
prevalence in TCGA-LUAD ($\sim$140 positive cases across 687 slides).
Increasing to 2{,}000--5{,}000 slides from multiple institutions
would stabilise fold-to-fold variance and likely improve population
AUROC above 0.70, particularly if the cohort includes mucinous/IMA
cases whose morphology is strongly KRAS-predictive but under-represented
in TCGA.
(ii)~\textbf{Expanded pattern taxonomy:} adding a mucinous/IMA class
to the six-pattern classifier would provide the Choquet integral with
the morphological feature most strongly associated with KRAS in the
literature~\cite{shim2015krasmucinous}, potentially improving both
population AUROC and per-slide interpretability.
(iii)~\textbf{Slide-level confidence calibration:} rather than
applying a single Conclusive/Inconclusive label to an entire gene,
a per-slide confidence score---incorporating the morphological profile
similarity to KRAS-positive training cases---could flag individual
cases as high-confidence even when the gene-level AUROC is
Inconclusive.
This would allow TCGA-55-7815 (96.5\%, morphologically consistent
with KRAS) to be reported as ``high individual confidence despite
population-level uncertainty,'' giving clinicians more nuanced
guidance.
(iv)~\textbf{Per-slide model selection:} The current system selects
the gene-optimal prediction method based on population-level AUROC
(Finding~2).
However, the per-slide ablation comparison reveals that the
population-optimal method is not always per-slide optimal: for
a given slide, a different model may produce a higher $P(\text{mut})$.
The Analysis tab makes this visible by displaying all three model
outputs side by side.
A future meta-learner could select the most appropriate model per
slide based on morphological profile, but this requires calibration
that the current dataset size ($N = 687$) does not support.

\subsection{Medium-Term Extensions (1--2 Years)}
\label{sec:disc:future:medium}

\textbf{Foundation model embeddings.}
Replacing CTransPath with more recent foundation model encoders (UNI, CONCH, GigaPath, Virchow) would test whether the pattern-informed approach provides an additional benefit on top of embeddings that are already optimised for diverse histopathological tasks.
If the Fuzzy Choquet branch still provides gene-specific advantages (as observed for KRAS and RBM10 with CTransPath) when paired with stronger foundation model encoders, this would be strong evidence that the fuzzy aggregation mechanism captures complementary inter-pattern information independent of the specific visual encoder.

\textbf{Dedicated Choquet-only ablation.}
The current Fuzzy Choquet MIL (condition~FC) is a hybrid dual-pathway
model that uses both 512-d CTransPath embeddings (via a shared ABMIL
encoder) and a parallel Choquet branch on 6-d pattern memberships.
A valuable ablation would evaluate a \emph{pure} Choquet model
operating on pattern probabilities alone, without the embedding pathway.
This would isolate the contribution of the fuzzy aggregation mechanism
and test whether the 21-parameter fuzzy measure can achieve meaningful
mutation prediction using only the explicit pattern representation,
independent of the visual embedding signal.
Such a comparison would clarify whether the Choquet branch's advantage
for KRAS and RBM10 stems from the inter-pattern interaction modelling
or from the complementary embedding information provided by the shared
encoder.
If a pure Choquet model achieves non-trivial AUROC on pattern features
alone, it would confirm that the interaction structure captured by the
fuzzy measure carries genuine mutation-discriminative signal---a
stronger claim than the current results, where the embedding pathway
may account for most of the predictive power.

\textbf{Multi-task and multi-gene learning.}
Training a single ABMIL model to jointly predict all six genes, with shared attention weights and gene-specific classification heads, could leverage known mutation co-occurrence patterns (e.g., KRAS--STK11 co-mutation).
The Choquet integral could be extended to model interactions between genes as well as between patterns.

\textbf{Expanded pattern taxonomy.}
Incorporating additional morphological features---tumour budding,
tumour--stroma ratio, lymphocyte infiltration density,
and a non-neoplastic tissue class (stroma, normal alveolar parenchyma)---as
additional channels in the pattern classifier would increase the information
content of the pattern representation.
The addition of a non-neoplastic class would be particularly relevant for
\textit{STK11} prediction, where the ``cold'' immune microenvironment
visible in stromal and peri-tumoural regions carries predictive signal that
the current six-class neoplastic taxonomy cannot capture directly.


\textbf{Fuzzy rule-based mutation priors.}
Constructing an explicit fuzzy inference system with IF-THEN rules encoding known clinicopathological associations (e.g., ``IF solid percentage IS high AND micropapillary percentage IS medium THEN TP53 risk IS elevated'') would create a fully transparent mutation prediction pathway that a pathologist can read, validate, and modify.
The fuzzy rule system output could serve as a prior probability concatenated with the slide-level representation before final classification.

\paragraph{Ontology and knowledge graph extension.}
The current knowledge graph is grounded in NCIt and MONDO IRIs
with PubMed-cited provenance for each edge.
Integration of additional ontologies (DOID, SNOMED~CT, ICD-O) and
cross-institution IRI alignment would improve coverage and
interoperability.
Formalising relations in OWL and evaluating OWL reasoners for
consistency checking and classification would move the system toward
a fully formal knowledge representation.
 
\paragraph{DeepSearch scaling and validation.}
The current DeepSearch pipeline processes 12 curated queries
(Table~\ref{tab:deepsearch_queries}) against three literature
sources.
Scaling to more queries and sources, strengthening entity linking
(currently dictionary-based), and implementing human-in-the-loop
review of discovered relations before graph incorporation would
increase the reliability and coverage of the enrichment layer.
 
\paragraph{Explanation quality metrics and KG evaluation.}
A central design decision of LCHAI is that the knowledge graph does
\emph{not} participate in model training or mutation prediction---it
operates purely downstream as a \textbf{grounding layer for
LLM-generated explanations}.
When the system produces a natural-language interpretation of a
prediction (e.g., ``TP53 mutation detected with high confidence;
TP53 is associated with solid/micropapillary morphology and responds
to checkpoint immunotherapy''), the KG provides the factual context
(gene--pattern associations, gene--pathway--drug chains, PMIDs) that
the LLM uses to construct its response.
Without this grounding, the LLM would generate explanations from its
general training data, risking \emph{hallucinated} clinical
associations---for example, inventing a drug indication that does not
exist or attributing a pattern to the wrong gene.
The KG thus serves as a curated factual anchor that constrains the
LLM's output to verified biomedical knowledge.

A second anti-hallucination mechanism operates at the \emph{linguistic}
level: all intensity terms (e.g., ``Moderate Synergy,'' ``Embedding-Led'')
are pre-computed by the fuzzy linguistic label system
(Section~\ref{sec:meth:interp:fuzzy_labels}) and injected verbatim
into the LLM prompt.
This dual grounding---KG for clinical facts, fuzzy labels for intensity
characterisations---ensures that the explanation is fully controlled by
the system and cannot diverge from the displayed numeric outputs.

Two aspects of this design remain to be formally evaluated:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{KG correctness and coverage.}
    Constructing a curated gold standard for pattern $\to$ gene $\to$
    treatment chains and reporting retrieval precision/recall would
    quantify how complete and accurate the KG's 30 curated edges and
    DeepSearch-discovered relations are.
    Comparing LLM-generated explanations \emph{with} KG grounding
    versus \emph{without} it (i.e., LLM using only its parametric
    knowledge) in a blinded clinician evaluation would directly
    measure the hallucination reduction benefit.

    \item \textbf{Prediction invariance.}
    Because the KG is downstream of the prediction pipeline, enabling
    or disabling the explanation layer should produce \emph{identical}
    mutation probabilities.
    A formal test confirming this invariance would verify that the
    ontology layer introduces no unintended feedback into the
    prediction, as intended by design.
\end{enumerate}
 

\textbf{Extension to other cancer types.}
The three-artefact pipeline (fuzzy margin classification $\to$
pattern-informed MIL $\to$ Choquet aggregation) is not inherently
specific to lung adenocarcinoma.
Applying the methodology to lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC),
colorectal adenocarcinoma, or gastric cancer---all of which have
WHO-defined growth pattern taxonomies---would test the generalisability
of the framework and provide a broader evidence base for fuzzy
representation learning in computational pathology.

\textbf{Multi-centre retrospective studies.}
Scaling beyond the 687 TCGA-LUAD slides to 2{,}000--5{,}000 slides
from multiple institutions would reduce fold-to-fold variance and
enable more powerful statistical comparisons between conditions.


\subsection{Long-Term Vision}
\label{sec:disc:future:long}

\textbf{Clinical deployment and prospective validation.}
The ultimate goal is a prospective clinical study in which LCHAI is deployed at a partner hospital and evaluated on incoming diagnostic cases.
EAGLE~\cite{campanella2025eagle} demonstrated that such prospective validation is achievable for EGFR prediction, with their silent trial on 197 patients reaching AUROC 0.890 and the potential to prevent unnecessary genetic testing in 43\% of cases.
While the present work does not approach that scale, it provides the interpretable framework within which such a deployment could be designed---with the advantage that clinicians can verify and override the model's reasoning through the pattern overlay.

\textbf{Fuzzy neuro-symbolic mutation prediction.}
A longer-term research direction is the development of a fully neuro-symbolic pipeline in which the pattern classifier provides symbolic labels (``solid,'' ``acinar''), the fuzzy logic engine reasons over these labels using domain-encoded rules and learned fuzzy measures, and the neural network provides the visual backbone.
This architecture would maximise interpretability while maintaining the flexibility of deep learning, representing a principled synthesis of the fuzzy and neural paradigms.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:disc:conclusion}

Table~\ref{tab:rq_summary_ch7} summarises the status of each research
question.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\small
\caption{Summary of research question outcomes.}
\label{tab:rq_summary_ch7}
\begin{tabular}{c l l}
\hline
\textbf{RQ} & \textbf{Core question} & \textbf{Outcome} \\
\hline
1 & Adaptive fuzzy loss improves pattern classification? & Met ($p = 0.001$, $d = 1.00$) \\
2 & Pattern-informed MIL improves mutation prediction?   & Partially met (gene-dependent) \\
3 & Fuzzy Choquet captures inter-pattern interactions?   & Met (interpretable Shapley values) \\
4 & Clinically meaningful explanations?                  & Met (6-level framework demonstrated) \\
5 & Greater benefit under data scarcity?                 & Partially met (competitive at $N{=}687$) \\
6 & Ontology-grounded explanations?                      & Met (KG with provenance tags) \\
7 & Knowledge graph enrichment?                          & Partially met (50 relations; no formal eval.) \\

\end{tabular}

This thesis has investigated the application of fuzzy margin representation
learning to the prediction of somatic mutations from H\&E-stained
whole-slide images of lung adenocarcinoma, under the constraint of limited
publicly available data.

Three computational artefacts were developed and evaluated within the
Design Science Research framework:

FuzzyArcLoss~V2, a novel angular margin loss function that adapts the
training margin per sample through an explicit fuzzy membership function,
achieving 92.31\% macro-F1 on six histological patterns---the highest
among 18 evaluated loss functions across 15 independent runs.
The method significantly outperforms SphereFace ($p = 0.001$,
Cohen's $d = 1.00$) and consistently leads all baselines, with particular
benefit on the minority cribriform class.

Pattern-Informed ABMIL, a multiple instance learning architecture that
fuses CTransPath visual embeddings with fuzzy pattern membership
probabilities, achieving mutation prediction AUROCs of 0.694--0.717 for
clinically actionable genes (EGFR, TP53) on 687 TCGA-LUAD slides
(668 unique patients).
The approach matches embedding-only ABMIL while enabling a six-level
interpretability framework: spatial attention maps, pattern overlays,
SHAP decomposition, Choquet Shapley values, per-slide feature
channel ablation, and fuzzy linguistic labels---that distinguishes what the model attends to from
what actually helps the prediction.

Fuzzy Choquet MIL, a hybrid architecture ($\sim$266{,}000 total
parameters) whose interpretable core---a 21-parameter 2-additive fuzzy
measure---replaces attention pooling with a Choquet integral over
pattern memberships in a parallel branch alongside a shared embedding
encoder.
The model achieves mean AUROC 0.653 across
six genes---competitive with the $\sim$200{,}000-parameter ABMIL
baseline---while leading for KRAS (0.609) and RBM10 (0.661), and
providing intrinsic interpretability through Shapley values and interaction
indices that align with known clinicopathological associations.

The ontology-grounded explanation layer---a three-tier knowledge graph
grounding predicted patterns and mutations in canonical IRIs from NCIt,
and MONDO with PMID-level provenance tagging---provides the fourth level
of the LCHAI~v2.0 instantiation.
Unlike the three computational artefacts (which operate on image
representations), the knowledge graph operates purely as a semantic
substrate downstream of prediction, linking computed outputs to curated
clinical knowledge and literature-discovered associations.
This separation of prediction from explanation ensures that
interpretability remains auditable and faithful to the model's
computations while being expressed in clinically recognisable terms.

The integrative LCHAI~v2.0 prototype demonstrates that these artefacts
compose into a functional clinical decision support system, from slide
processing through mutation prediction to explainable output with
therapeutic knowledge linkage.

The results are competitive with published methods at comparable data
scale: this thesis surpasses Saldanha~et~al.\ (2023, $\sim$676 slides)
and Logan~et~al.\ (2022, $\sim$747 slides) for TP53 and EGFR, while
providing interpretability that neither study offers.
The gap relative to large-scale proprietary studies (DeepGEM, EAGLE,
CHIEF) reflects the fundamental data asymmetry between public academic
research and hospital-scale industrial efforts---a gap that is structural
rather than methodological.

The principal insight of this thesis is that the value of fuzzy logic in
computational pathology extends beyond a single loss function.
When applied coherently across training (FuzzyArcLoss), representation
(soft membership probabilities), and aggregation (Choquet integral),
fuzzy logic provides a principled framework for modelling the inherent
gradience of biological tissue while maintaining interpretability.
The utility of this framework is gene-dependent---soft fuzzy
representations benefit genes with diffuse morphological correlates,
while crisp representations suffice for genes with morphologically specific
associations---and this gene-specificity itself constitutes an analytical
contribution that connects the formalism of fuzzy set theory to the
biology of cancer genomics.

A key finding that emerged from the representative case analysis
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:interp:enrichment}) is that the interpretability
framework is \emph{most valuable when the model fails}.
For the two true positives (TP53 and KRAS), the explanations are
confirmatory---the clinician would have ordered molecular testing
regardless of the AI's reasoning.
For the four false negatives (EGFR, STK11, KEAP1, RBM10), the
explanations are \emph{critical}: they distinguish between a
confidently negative prediction and one where the model lacked
morphological signal, enabling the clinician to make an informed
decision about whether to pursue molecular testing.
The Conclusive/Inconclusive framework validated this design:
all four false negatives were correctly labelled Inconclusive,
meaning the system warned clinicians \emph{before} they saw the
prediction that the gene's model has limited discriminative power.
No prior mutation prediction system provides this level of
quantitative failure explanation---the ability not only to predict but
to explain its own limitations.

\paragraph{Practical clinical value.}
While LCHAI is not intended to replace molecular testing, the
evaluation suggests three concrete near-term uses:
(i)~\textbf{triage for molecular testing}---in settings where
sequencing has a 7--21 day turnaround, a Conclusive high-probability
prediction (e.g., TP53 at 95\%) could prioritise patients for
expedited testing;
(ii)~\textbf{medical education}---residents can use the overlay,
attention map, and SHAP decomposition to learn morphology--genotype
relationships interactively; and
(iii)~\textbf{research framework}---the architecture
(FuzzyArcLoss + ABMIL + Choquet + KG) is transferable to other
cancer types with WHO-defined growth pattern taxonomies (LUSC,
colorectal, gastric).

\paragraph{The micropapillary--acinar discrepancy.}
A preliminary expert validation by a board-certified pathologist
from SOLCA Ecuador (Section~\ref{sec:eval:pattern:confusion})
revealed that in five of eight evaluated TCGA subregions, the model
predicted micropapillary where the pathologist identified acinar.
Two non-mutually-exclusive explanations apply:
(i)~domain shift between the ANORAK training cohort (TRACERx, UK
institutions) and the TCGA inference slides (US institutions),
where differences in staining protocols, scanning equipment, and
tissue preparation may alter visual pattern boundaries; and
(ii)~the low inter-observer agreement for micropapillary reported in
the ANORAK paper ($\rho = 0.35$--$0.44$)~\cite{anorak2024},
indicating that the boundary between these two patterns is
inherently subjective even among expert pathologists.
This discrepancy does \emph{not} invalidate the tile-level
classifier on its training domain (where acinar achieves 100\%
recall and micropapillary 77\% in the confusion matrix,
Figure~\ref{fig:confusion_matrix}), but it underscores the need for
multi-cohort validation and the active learning mechanism
implemented in LCHAI~v2.0.

In conclusion, the principal contribution of this thesis is not
predicting mutations with clinical-grade accuracy---that would
require cohorts of 60{,}000+ slides as in CHIEF or
GigaPath---but demonstrating that \textbf{fuzzy logic can improve
pattern classification, capture inter-pattern interactions via the
Choquet integral, and make the entire prediction pipeline transparent
through multiple layers of explanation}, all with 687 public slides.
The approach does not compete with the brute-force scaling of foundation
models but offers a complementary paradigm: one in which a pathologist can
understand, verify, and \emph{correct} the model's reasoning.
As computational pathology moves from research prototypes toward clinical
deployment, the ability to explain predictions---and to explain
failures---in terms that clinicians recognise may prove as important as
the predictions themselves.
