\chapter{Evaluation of Artifacts}
\label{ch:evaluation}

This chapter presents the quantitative and qualitative evaluation of the three computational artifacts developed in this thesis.
Following the Design Science Research methodology, each artefact is evaluated against its baselines using the protocol specified in Section~\ref{sec:meth:evaluation_protocol}, and the results are interpreted in terms of the research questions defined in Section~\ref{sec:uc:clinical_questions}.

The evaluation is organised in three parts.
Section~\ref{sec:eval:pattern} evaluates Artifact~1 (FuzzyArcLoss~V2 for pattern classification).
Section~\ref{sec:eval:mutation} evaluates Artifacts~2 and~3 (PI-ABMIL and FC-MIL for mutation prediction).
Section~\ref{sec:eval:literature} compares the results against the published literature.
Section~\ref{sec:eval:interpretability} presents the qualitative interpretability analysis.
Section~\ref{sec:eval:rq} synthesises the findings by answering each research question.

\subsection{Single-Run Results: Screening of 18 Loss Functions}
\label{sec:eval:pattern:singlerun}

Prior to the rigorous K-fold evaluation, all 18 loss function variants
(Table~\ref{tab:loss_variants}) were screened on a single 80/20
train/test split (509 training tiles, 128 test tiles) to identify the
top performers for the computationally expensive multi-seed
cross-validation.
The key finding was a universal improvement of approximately 18 percentage
points in macro-F1 across \emph{all} 18 variants, from a range of
71--76\% before the pipeline corrections documented in
Section~\ref{sec:uc:pipeline_fixes} to 89--95\% after corrections.
This universal improvement confirms that the dominant performance gains
were attributable to the pipeline fixes (4-channel mask input, fp32
precision, margin-free evaluation logits) rather than to any specific
loss function.

Table~\ref{tab:singlerun_all18} reports the single-run macro-F1 for all
18 loss functions within the corrected pipeline.
These results are based on a single data split of 128 test tiles and
are therefore subject to high variance; they serve as a screening step
to select the top~5 methods for the definitive K-fold evaluation
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:pattern:kfold}).

Two observations from the single-run screening motivated the K-fold
evaluation design.
First, SphereFace ranked first (95.03\%) and FuzzyArcLoss~V2 second
(94.48\%), separated by only 0.55~pp on 128 test samples---well within
the expected run-to-run variance of $\pm 0.8$~pp.
The question of which method truly generalises better could not be
resolved without multi-run evaluation.
Second, the top~5 methods spanned three categories: fixed-margin
(SphereFace, ArcFace), fuzzy-adaptive (FuzzyArcLoss~V2, V3~SubCenters),
and non-angular (Softmax~CE), ensuring a comprehensive comparison across
paradigms.

\textbf{Selection criterion for K-fold.}
The top~5 methods were selected based on two principles:
(a)~coverage of all three loss paradigms (fixed angular margin,
fuzzy-adaptive angular margin, and standard cross-entropy), and
(b)~the top single-run performer from each paradigm.
AdaptiveFace and FuzzyArcLoss~V2~ClassParams were excluded despite
strong single-run results because they are conceptual variants of the
methods already selected (ArcFace and V2~Optuna, respectively), and the
computational cost of 15 runs per method ($\sim$2.2~hours each)
constrained the number of methods.

\label{sec:eval:pattern:kfold}

repeated with 3 random seeds (42, 123, 2026), yielding 15 independent

Table~\ref{tab:kfold_f1} reports the final rankings.

\textbf{Key finding: ranking reversal.}
FuzzyArcLoss~V2 achieves the highest mean macro-F1 of $92.31\% \pm 2.04$
across 15 runs, while SphereFace---which appeared to be the top performer
in the single-run screening (Table~\ref{tab:singlerun_all18})---drops to
last place with $90.03\% \pm 2.51$.
SphereFace's single-run result of 95.03\% was a favourable split artefact
that the multi-run evaluation corrects by a full 5 percentage points.
This reversal underscores the necessity of multi-run evaluation on small
datasets: any conclusion drawn from a single split of 128 test samples
would have been misleading.

The comparison between V2 and SphereFace is statistically significant
($p = 0.001$) with a large effect size (Cohen's $d = 1.00$) and V2
winning 14 of 15 runs.
SphereFace's large fixed multiplicative margin ($m = 4$) produces the
highest variance among all methods (std = 2.51), suggesting that its
aggressive margin destabilises training on certain data splits.
The single run where SphereFace outperformed V2 (seed=123, fold=3:
SphereFace 92.4\% vs V2 91.7\%) was the only fold where SphereFace's
aggressive margin happened to benefit rather than harm generalisation.
FuzzyArcLoss~V2's adaptive membership, which reduces the penalty for
ambiguous samples near class boundaries, produces more consistent
performance across splits.

The differences between V2 and ArcFace ($+$0.14~pp, $p = 0.651$),
V3~SubCenters ($+$0.51~pp, $p = 0.148$), and Softmax~CE ($+$0.78~pp,
$p = 0.138$) are not statistically significant at $p < 0.05$.
However, the consistent ranking---V2 first in all comparisons---and the
directional advantage across 9--10 of 15 runs suggest a real but small
effect that the 15-run protocol lacks power to confirm.
With $n = 15$ paired observations, the minimum detectable effect at
80\% power and $\alpha = 0.05$ is approximately $d \approx 0.78$,
meaning that only large effects (such as the V2-vs-SphereFace gap) can
be reliably detected.

Three findings emerge from the per-class analysis.

\textbf{First, FuzzyArcLoss~V2 achieves the highest F1 on the
cribriform class} (83.6\% versus ArcFace's 81.4\% and SphereFace's
76.1\%).
Cribriform is a minority class with only $\sim$55 training samples and
morphological features that overlap substantially with acinar (from
which it is derived as a high-grade variant).
The $+2.2$~pp advantage over ArcFace and $+7.5$~pp over SphereFace on
this class is consistent with V2's design principle: the adaptive fuzzy
membership reduces the angular margin for low-confidence samples, which
disproportionately benefits underrepresented classes where borderline
samples constitute a larger fraction of the training set.
SphereFace's 76.1\% cribriform F1---combined with the highest per-class
standard deviation (12.8)---indicates that its fixed multiplicative
margin of $m = 4$ is too aggressive for this data-scarce class,
producing unreliable representations across splits.

\textbf{Second, the acinar and lepidic classes exceed 93\% F1 across all
methods}, indicating that these morphologically distinctive patterns are
well-separated in the embedding space regardless of the loss function.
Acinar morphology (round-to-oval glandular structures with central
lumina) and lepidic morphology (``wallpaper-like'' growth along
pre-existing alveolar walls) have highly distinctive visual signatures
that even the simplest softmax classifier captures reliably.

\textbf{Third, the differentiation among methods concentrates on the
harder classes.}
The inter-method F1 range (best minus worst) is 7.5~pp for cribriform,
3.7~pp for micropapillary, and only 0.8~pp for solid.
This confirms that the choice of loss function matters most where
morphological boundaries are ambiguous and training data is scarce---
precisely the regime for which FuzzyArcLoss~V2 was designed.


\subsubsection{Optuna Hyperparameter Analysis}
\label{sec:eval:pattern:optuna}

The Optuna Bayesian search over 50 trials identified the optimal
configuration $s^{*} = 46.1$, $m^{*} = 0.518$, $\tau^{*} = 0.448$
for FuzzyArcLoss~V2.
The V2 Optuna model achieved avgPR = 0.9306 on the screening split,
outperforming the V3~SubCenters Optuna search
(avgPR = 0.9127) by $+1.8$ percentage points.

The convergence of the margin $m$ to a value close to the ArcFace
default ($m = 0.5$) while the scale $s$ diverged significantly
($46.1$ versus the default $30$) confirms that the performance advantage
of V2 arises from the \emph{modulation mechanism} rather than from the
raw margin magnitude.
A higher scale sharpens the softmax distribution, making the model more
decisive.
In standard ArcFace, this would risk training instability for ambiguous
samples.
In FuzzyArcLoss~V2, the fuzzy membership acts as a ``safety valve'': the
reduced margin for uncertain samples ($\mu_{i} \approx 0.3$--$0.5$)
compensates for the aggressive scale, preventing gradient explosion at
decision boundaries.
The Optuna search also confirmed that Label Smoothing~$+$~Focal weighting
is the optimal auxiliary loss for V2 (all top-4 trials agreed), consistent
with the theoretical complementarity of output-level label smoothing and
embedding-level fuzzy margin softening.


\subsubsection{Summary of Pattern Classification Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval:pattern:summary}

The 15-run K-fold evaluation resolves the ambiguity of the single-run
screening and yields the following conclusions for RQ1:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item FuzzyArcLoss~V2 ranks first with 92.31\% mean macro-F1,
    consistently ahead of all baselines across 15 independent runs.

    \item The advantage over SphereFace is statistically significant
    ($p = 0.001$, Cohen's $d = 1.00$, 14/15 wins), establishing that
    V2's fuzzy-adaptive margin produces reliably better generalisation
    than SphereFace's aggressive fixed multiplicative margin on this
    small dataset.

    \item V2 ranks first in \emph{every} pairwise comparison: $+$0.14~pp
    over ArcFace, $+$0.51~pp over V3~SubCenters, and $+$0.78~pp over
    Softmax~CE, winning 9--10 of 15 runs in each case.
    These differences do not reach $p < 0.05$ because with only
    $n = 15$ paired observations, the protocol can only detect large
    effects ($d \geq 0.78$); the consistent directional advantage
    across all comparisons is nonetheless informative.

    \item V2's greatest per-class advantage occurs on the hardest class
    (cribriform: 83.6\%, $+$2.2~pp over ArcFace, $+$7.5~pp over SphereFace),
    directly supporting the design hypothesis that adaptive fuzzy margins
    benefit underrepresented classes with ambiguous morphological boundaries.

    \item The systematic pipeline corrections contributed the largest
    absolute gains ($+$18~pp across all methods), demonstrating that
    implementation details---particularly 4-channel mask input, fp32
    precision, and margin-free evaluation logits---are as critical as
    algorithmic innovation when applying angular margin losses to small
    histopathological datasets.
\end{enumerate}

Three observations emerge from the confusion matrix:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Acinar, solid, and lepidic are perfectly classified}
    (36/36, 21/21, and 16/16 respectively), indicating that the model
    has learned discriminative features for these morphologically
    distinct patterns.

    \item \textbf{Cribriform is the most confused class} (12/19
    correct; per-class F1\,=\,0.63), primarily misclassified as acinar
    (4~tiles) or solid (3~tiles).
    This is morphologically plausible: cribriform is defined as a
    high-grade acinar variant with sieve-like glandular
    structures~\cite{who2021thoracic}, and its boundary with complex
    acinar and solid patterns is a known source of inter-observer
    disagreement~\cite{thunnissen2014reproducibility}.

    \item \textbf{Micropapillary--papillary confusion} (3~tiles of
    micropapillary classified as papillary; per-class F1\,=\,0.77)
    aligns with the ANORAK paper's finding that micropapillary has the
    lowest inter-observer agreement among pathologists
    ($\rho = 0.35$--$0.44$)~\cite{anorak2024}.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Preliminary expert validation on TCGA slides.}
A board-certified pathologist from SOLCA (Sociedad de Lucha Contra el
C\'{a}ncer del Ecuador) independently reviewed eight subregions from
five TCGA slides processed through LCHAI~v2.0.
Of these, one case (TCGA-49-AAR9, solid-dominant) matched exactly,
and one subregion (acinar) also matched.
However, in five subregions the expert identified \emph{acinar} morphology
where the model predicted \emph{micropapillary}, and in one subregion
the expert found the tissue ``non-assessable'' while the model
predicted micropapillary.

This pattern of disagreement is consistent with two known phenomena:
(i)~\textbf{domain shift} between the ANORAK training cohort
(TRACERx, UK institutions) and the TCGA inference cohort (US
institutions), where differences in scanner hardware, staining
protocols, and tissue preparation may alter the visual features of
pattern boundaries even though both pipelines deliver
$224 \times 224$\,px tiles to the model; and
(ii)~the \textbf{high inter-observer variability} for micropapillary
and acinar patterns reported in the ANORAK
paper~\cite{anorak2024}---the two patterns most frequently
confused in the expert review are precisely those with the lowest
pathologist agreement in the literature ($\rho = 0.35$--$0.44$).

Critically, this preliminary expert review \emph{does not invalidate}
the tile-level classifier on ANORAK data (where acinar achieves
perfect classification and the overall Macro-F1 is 89.2\%), but
highlights that
\textbf{pattern-level predictions on out-of-distribution slides
should be interpreted with caution} and accompanied by pathologist
verification---which is the intended clinical workflow in LCHAI~v2.0.

\subsection{Finding 1: Pattern-Informed Approaches Match Embedding-Only ABMIL}
\label{sec:eval:mutation:finding1}

The central hypothesis of this thesis (RQ2) is that injecting fuzzy pattern
information improves mutation prediction.
Table~\ref{tab:p_vs_b2} compares PI-ABMIL (PI) against the
embedding-only baseline (B2) gene by gene.

\begin{table}[ht!]
\centering
\caption{Gene-level comparison: PI-ABMIL (PI) versus ABMIL embeddings (B2).
$\Delta = \text{PI} - \text{B2}$.}
\label{tab:p_vs_b2}
\begin{tabular}{l c c c}
\hline
\textbf{Gene} & \textbf{B2 (emb)} & \textbf{PI-ABMIL} & \textbf{$\Delta$} \\
\hline
TP53  & 0.718 & 0.717 & $-$0.001 \\
EGFR  & 0.701 & 0.694 & $-$0.007 \\
STK11 & 0.684 & 0.695 & $+$0.011 \\
KEAP1 & 0.597 & 0.610 & $+$0.013 \\
KRAS  & 0.607 & 0.590 & $-$0.017 \\
RBM10 & 0.642 & 0.640 & $-$0.002 \\
\hline
\textbf{Mean} & \textbf{0.658} & \textbf{0.658} & \phantom{$-$}0.000 \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

PI-ABMIL is essentially tied with B2 on the overall mean
(0.658 versus 0.658).
The deltas are within the fold-to-fold standard deviation for every gene,
indicating that none of the differences is statistically significant.
PI-ABMIL neither harms nor substantially improves
AUROC.

\textbf{Interpretation.}
The conclusion is that fuzzy pattern probabilities \emph{preserve}
performance while adding the interpretability pathway through histological
patterns.
The CTransPath embeddings already encode the morphological information that
the pattern probabilities provide; the concatenation is informationally
redundant but not harmful.
The value of the pattern channel is therefore primarily \emph{interpretive}
rather than \emph{discriminative}: it enables the six-level interpretability
framework (Section~\ref{sec:eval:interpretability}) without sacrificing
prediction quality.

\textbf{Important caveat: population-level vs.\ per-slide behaviour.}
The AUROC equivalence above is a \emph{population-level} statement
averaged over 687~slides.
It does not imply that patterns are neutral on every individual slide.
The per-slide ablation comparison displayed in LCHAI~v2.0
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:interp:enrichment}) reveals that on some slides
the pattern channel substantially \emph{helps} the prediction while on
others it substantially \emph{hurts} it---for example, on one TP53
case the combined model yields $P(\text{mut}) = 79.0\%$ while the
embedding-only model yields $95.1\%$ ($-16.1$\,pp).
These per-slide deviations cancel out in aggregate, producing the
identical population-level AUROC.
This is precisely the motivation for the per-slide ablation
(Level~5 of the interpretability framework): it warns the clinician
when the pattern channel is constructive or destructive for a specific
slide, rather than relying on the population average.

\subsection{Finding 2: The Optimal Representation Is Gene-Dependent}
\label{sec:eval:mutation:finding2}

No single condition dominates across all genes.
Table~\ref{tab:gene_winners} identifies the top condition per gene.

\label{tab:gene_winners}
\begin{tabular}{l l c l}
\hline
\textbf{Gene} & \textbf{Best condition} & \textbf{AUROC} &

\hline
TP53  & B2 (embeddings) & 0.718 & Visual features sufficient \\
EGFR  & B2 (embeddings) & 0.701 & Visual features sufficient \\

\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The results stratify into three distinct groups.
B2 (embedding-only ABMIL) leads for TP53 and EGFR, where mutations correlate
with well-defined morphological signatures~\cite{leighl2002tp53morphology,yoshizawa2013egfrmorph} already encoded compactly in the
CTransPath feature space.
PI-ABMIL (PI) leads for STK11 and KEAP1, the two
genes with the most diffuse morphological associations---where, at the
\emph{population level}, the explicit pattern probabilities provide signal
that is less efficiently extracted by the generic embeddings alone.
Note that per-slide ablation analysis (Section~\ref{sec:eval:interp:enrichment})
reveals that this population-level benefit does not hold for every
individual slide: on the representative cases, patterns actively degrade
STK11 ($-5.5$\,pp) and KEAP1 ($-44.7$\,pp) predictions.
FC-MIL (FC) leads for KRAS and RBM10, where inter-pattern
interactions modelled by the Choquet integral appear to capture relevant
co-occurrence structure.

This gene-dependent pattern reflects the biological heterogeneity of the
mutation--morphology associations:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{TP53 and EGFR}: Mutations correlate with specific,
    well-defined patterns (solid/micropapillary for TP53~\cite{leighl2002tp53morphology}; lepidic/papillary
    for EGFR~\cite{yoshizawa2013egfrmorph}) that the CTransPath embeddings encode efficiently.
    Adding explicit pattern probabilities introduces no new information for
    these genes.

    \item \textit{STK11 and KEAP1}: Mutations produce diffuse morphological
    changes across multiple pattern types.
    For STK11 ($+$0.011) and KEAP1 ($+$0.013), the explicit pattern
    probabilities provide a modest but consistent advantage over
    embedding-only ABMIL.
    Notably, for KEAP1, neither FC-MIL (0.589, $-$0.008 vs B2) nor
    ablation one-hot (0.594) matches PI-ABMIL's soft-probability
    concatenation, suggesting that the continuous fuzzy representation
    captures inter-pattern gradients that crisp labels and Choquet
    aggregation do not.

    \item \textit{KRAS and RBM10}: The Choquet integral's capacity to model
    multiplicative interactions between pattern memberships provides
    a small but consistent advantage.
    The learned interaction indices reveal that the co-presence of
    lepidic and solid patterns within the same slide is synergistic
    for KRAS prediction ($I = +0.032$)---not because KRAS is
    clinically associated with lepidic (it is not; KRAS correlates
    with solid and mucinous/IMA morphology~\cite{shim2015krasmucinous}), but because the
    model has learned that the simultaneous presence of these
    minority patterns within a micropapillary-dominant slide
    carries discriminative signal that singleton pattern
    contributions miss.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Finding 3: Fuzzy Versus Crisp Pattern Representations}
\label{sec:eval:mutation:finding3}

The comparison between PI-ABMIL (PI, soft fuzzy probabilities)
and the ablation A (hard one-hot labels) tests whether the continuous fuzzy
representation is more informative than its discrete counterpart.

\begin{table}[ht!]
\centering
\caption{PI-ABMIL (PI, fuzzy) versus Ablation (A, crisp) pattern representations.}
\label{tab:fuzzy_vs_crisp}
\begin{tabular}{l c c c l}
\hline
\textbf{Gene} & \textbf{PI (fuzzy)} & \textbf{A (crisp)} &
\textbf{$\Delta$} & \textbf{Better} \\
\hline
TP53  & 0.717 & 0.716 & $+$0.001 & Fuzzy \\
EGFR  & 0.694 & 0.695 & $-$0.001 & Crisp \\
STK11 & 0.695 & 0.691 & $+$0.004 & Fuzzy \\
KEAP1 & 0.610 & 0.594 & $+$0.016 & Fuzzy \\

RBM10 & 0.640 & 0.623 & $+$0.017 & Fuzzy \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The fuzzy representation wins on four of six genes.
The most pronounced difference occurs for KEAP1 ($+$0.016) and RBM10
($+$0.017), both genes associated with diffuse or heterogeneous morphological
changes spanning multiple pattern types where inter-pattern boundary
uncertainty carries diagnostic information that hard labels discard.

For EGFR the difference is negligible ($-$0.001), consistent with its
well-defined morphological correlates (lepidic, papillary) that are
adequately captured by crisp classification.
For KRAS the difference is also negligible ($-$0.004); however, this
does \emph{not} imply that patterns are unimportant for KRAS---Finding~2
showed that FC-MIL (FC) leads all conditions for KRAS,
exploiting \emph{inter-pattern interactions} via the Choquet integral
rather than individual pattern membership softness.
KRAS benefits from the Choquet aggregation mechanism
(Finding~2) but not from fuzzy-versus-crisp softness (this finding),
because its primary morphological correlate (mucinous/IMA) is absent
from the 6-class taxonomy and the discriminative signal comes from
pattern \emph{co-occurrence} rather than boundary uncertainty.

The gene-specific finding---that the utility of fuzzy versus crisp pattern
representations is largest for genes with diffuse or heterogeneous
mutation--morphology associations---is an original analytical contribution
of this thesis.

\subsection{Finding 4: Patterns Alone Are Insufficient}
\label{sec:eval:mutation:finding4}

Condition B3 (ABMIL on pattern probabilities only, 6 dimensions) provides a
direct test of whether the six histological patterns carry sufficient
information for mutation prediction without the visual embeddings.

B3 achieves AUROCs of 0.545--0.653 across genes, consistently below B2
(0.597--0.718).
The KRAS result (0.545) is particularly striking: pattern probabilities
alone are nearly random for KRAS prediction.
This is explained by the absence of a Mucinous invasive (IMA) class in the ANORAK
taxonomy---the primary morphological correlate of KRAS mutations is
IMA morphology, which is not represented among the six ANORAK
classes.
The spatial and textural information encoded in the embeddings is
essential for KRAS prediction.

Two exceptions are worth noting.
B3 outperforms B2 for RBM10 (0.653 vs 0.642), the lowest-prevalence gene
(5.4\%), where the reduced dimensionality of B3 may act as implicit
regularisation.
B3 also approaches B2 for EGFR (0.625 vs 0.701), consistent with EGFR's
well-defined pattern correlates.
Nevertheless, the systematic underperformance of B3 relative to B2 confirms
that pattern probabilities alone cannot substitute for visual embeddings.

B3 outperforms B1 (XGBoost on slide-level pattern aggregates) for five of
six genes, with EGFR as the exception (B1~$= 0.634$ vs.\ B3~$= 0.625$).
The EGFR exception is notable: XGBoost on slide-level pattern aggregates
captures the EGFR--lepidic association via simple feature engineering,
while the ABMIL attention mechanism on tile-level patterns does not
improve upon this for EGFR.
For all other genes, the ABMIL attention mechanism extracts more
information from tile-level pattern distributions than simple
slide-level averaging does.

\subsection{Finding 5: The XGBoost Baseline Is Not Near-Random}
\label{sec:eval:mutation:finding5}

Condition B1 achieves AUROCs of 0.474--0.634 across genes.
The results are more heterogeneous than initially anticipated.
EGFR at 0.634 is the strongest B1 result---unexpectedly competitive---
reflecting the fact that EGFR mutations are tightly associated with
lepidic-predominant morphology~\cite{yoshizawa2013egfrmorph}, whose slide-level frequency is directly
captured by the XGBoost feature vector.
At the other extreme, RBM10 (0.474, below random) and KEAP1 (0.489) confirm
that for genes with diffuse or complex morphological correlates, naive
pattern-frequency aggregation fails entirely.
The overall mean of 0.530 validates the necessity of attention-based deep
learning for this task; however, the EGFR result suggests that for select
genes with strong single-pattern correlates, a lightweight XGBoost
classifier may be a practical clinical approximation.

\subsection{Finding 6: FC-MIL---Competitive Performance on
Gene-Specific Tasks}
\label{sec:eval:mutation:finding6}

FC-MIL (FC) achieves a mean AUROC of 0.653, comparable
to PI-ABMIL (0.658) and embedding-only ABMIL (0.658), and
is the sole top performer for KRAS (0.609) and RBM10 (0.661).
This is a notable result.
FC-MIL is a hybrid dual-pathway model ($\sim$266{,}000 trainable parameters)
that combines the same ABMIL encoder and attention mechanism as B2
(operating on 512-d CTransPath embeddings) with a parallel Choquet
integral branch that aggregates the 6-d pattern probabilities via a
21-parameter fuzzy measure.
The comparison FC-MIL~vs.~B2 is therefore not ``Choquet-only vs.\ ABMIL''
but rather ``ABMIL~$+$~Choquet branch vs.\ ABMIL-only'': when
FC-MIL~$\approx$~B2 in AUROC, the interpretation is that the Choquet branch
does not significantly improve discriminative accuracy but provides
intrinsic interpretability through its Shapley values and interaction
indices.
Specifically, when the learned Shapley values are near-uniform ($\phi_k \approx 1/6$ for all $k$) and interaction indices are small ($|I_{jk}| \ll 0.1$), the fuzzy measure is approximately additive and the Choquet integral reduces to a simple weighted average of pattern scores---meaning the model has not found meaningful pattern-level preferences for that gene, and the predictive signal originates from the shared embedding pathway.

FC-MIL's advantage for KRAS ($+$0.002 over B2) and RBM10 ($+$0.019 over B2)
is consistent with the hypothesis that, for these genes, the Choquet
branch's capacity to model superadditive interactions between pattern
types adds a small discriminative contribution beyond what the shared
ABMIL encoder alone captures.
The interaction between lepidic and solid patterns for KRAS ($I = +0.032$),
and between solid and acinar patterns ($I = +0.025$), are plausible
candidates for such synergistic effects.

For KEAP1---the gene most commonly cited as a candidate for interaction-based
modelling given its diffuse morphological signature---FC-MIL (0.589) is
outperformed by both B2 (0.597) and PI-ABMIL (0.610).
This negative result is instructive: KEAP1's diffuseness manifests as
a \emph{spread of probability mass across all six patterns} rather than
as a superadditive interaction between any two specific patterns.
The Choquet integral, which models multiplicative co-occurrence of high-scoring
patterns, is not the right inductive bias for a gene whose discriminative
signal lies in low-confidence, multi-pattern uncertainty.
PI-ABMIL, which preserves the full probability vector
without imposing any interaction structure, is better suited to this
diffuse regime.

FC-MIL's higher fold-to-fold variance (std 0.037--0.080) compared to the ABMIL
conditions (std 0.023--0.082) reflects the sensitivity of the 21-parameter
fuzzy measure in the Choquet branch, whose small parameter count makes it
more responsive to the specific composition of each fold.

\section{Interpretability Analysis}
\label{sec:eval:interpretability}
% ============================================================================

This section presents the qualitative interpretability evidence for the
PI-ABMIL approach (RQ4), based on attention maps
generated from the trained model checkpoints.
Table~\ref{tab:viz_folds} reports the AUROC values used by LCHAI~v2.0
for Conclusive/Inconclusive labelling.
By design, LCHAI uses the \emph{5-fold mean AUROC} rather than the
best single-fold AUROC.
This is a deliberate conservative choice grounded in statistical
generalisability: the mean of five held-out test folds is an estimator
of the \emph{expected performance on any unseen patient from the
same population}.
Each fold evaluates a different 20\% of patients that the model never
saw during training; averaging these five estimates converges toward
the true population-level AUROC.
In contrast, the best fold AUROC reflects a single favourable data
partition---it indicates what the model \emph{can} achieve under
ideal conditions but may overestimate performance for the next
patient, who may resemble the worst fold rather than the best.
For a clinical decision-support tool, conservative estimates are
preferable: a clinician who trusts a label based on inflated AUROC
may forgo molecular testing that would have been warranted.
For transparency, the best fold AUROC is reported alongside the mean
to show the performance ceiling and to motivate future work with
larger cohorts where the gap between mean and best narrows.

The best single-fold AUROC is shown for reference.}

\subsection{Attention Maps with Pattern Overlay}
\label{sec:eval:interp:attention}

The LCHAI~v2.0 six-level interpretability framework provides, for each
slide, complementary visualisations presented
in Figures~\ref{fig:attn_tp53_tp}--\ref{fig:attn_rbm10_tp}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pattern overlay} (left panel in each figure): maps
    every extracted tile to one of the six ANORAK-annotated growth patterns using a
    categorical colour code consistent with LCHAI~v2.0 (green~= micropapillary,
    red~= acinar, dark red~= solid, cyan~= cribriform, yellow~= papillary,
    blue~= lepidic), providing a spatial view of tumour heterogeneity.\footnote{Pattern names and colours follow the Zenodo--ANORAK scheme (Section~\ref{sec:uc:zenodo}). A preliminary expert review of TCGA slides by a board-certified pathologist from SOLCA Ecuador is reported in Section~\ref{sec:eval:pattern:confusion}; a formal multi-rater concordance study remains as future work (Section~\ref{sec:disc:lim:data}).}

    \item \textbf{ABMIL attention map} (right panel): shows the
    continuous attention weights assigned by the gene-specific ABMIL
    model, with cyan semi-transparent regions and white contours
    delineating the tiles that contribute most to the mutation
    prediction.
\end{itemize}

Each figure is arranged in a $2 \times 2$ grid with four
complementary panels:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Top left --- Pattern overlay}: spatial map of the six
    growth patterns.
    \item \textbf{Top right --- Attention map}: ABMIL attention weights
    showing which tiles drive the prediction.
    \item \textbf{Bottom left --- SHAP decomposition}: stacked bar
    showing the embedding/pattern attribution split (Level~3,
    Section~\ref{sec:meth:interp:shap}).
    \item \textbf{Bottom right --- Ablation comparison}: per-slide
    feature channel ablation (Level~5,
    Section~\ref{sec:meth:interp:ablation}), comparing the mutation
    probability produced by three separately trained models---Combined
    ($P_{\text{comb}}$), Embeddings-only ($P_{\text{emb}}$), and
    Patterns-only ($P_{\text{pat}}$)---on the same tile set.
    The ablation delta $\Delta_{\text{pat}} = P_{\text{comb}} -
    P_{\text{emb}}$ (Equation~\ref{eq:delta_pattern}) reveals whether
    adding pattern features helps ($\Delta > 0$) or hurts
    ($\Delta < 0$) the prediction on this specific slide.
\end{itemize}

The top panels reveal \emph{where} the model attends and \emph{what}
it finds there.
The bottom panels address a deeper question: SHAP (bottom left) shows
how the model \emph{distributes its internal attribution} between
embeddings and patterns, while ablation (bottom right) shows whether
patterns \emph{actually help or harm} the prediction
(cf.\ Section~\ref{sec:bg:channel_ablation}).
As the per-gene analyses below demonstrate, these two signals can
diverge dramatically.

All visualisations in this section were generated by LCHAI~v2.0
running locally on the trained checkpoints (Table~\ref{tab:viz_folds}),
using the same CTransPath tile embeddings and pattern probabilities
as the deployed system at \texttt{https://lchai.gptfy.biz}.

\textbf{SHAP reproducibility note:} Because DeepSHAP is a
gradient-based approximation, the exact embedding/pattern percentages
may vary by $\pm$5--10 percentage points across different analysis
runs on the same slide.
The \emph{directional interpretation}---whether a gene is
embedding-dominated, pattern-dominated, or balanced---is robust
and consistent across runs.
The percentages reported below and in
Table~\ref{tab:enrichment_summary} correspond to a single
deterministic run with fixed seed (\texttt{seed=42}).

\textbf{Interpretation.}
This case illustrates a fundamental limitation: \textbf{not every
mutation leaves a visible trace in the tissue}.
DNA sequencing confirms that this patient carries an EGFR mutation,
yet the model assigns only P(mut)~$= 0.076$---a \textbf{false
negative}.
Three factors explain why:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{The expected tissue patterns are absent.}
    EGFR mutations typically produce lepidic and papillary growth
    patterns~\cite{yoshizawa2013egfrmorph}---orderly, gland-like
    structures.
    However, this slide is 94.94\% micropapillary (a different,
    more aggressive pattern) with virtually no lepidic (0.3\%) or
    papillary (0.05\%) content.
    The visual ``fingerprint'' the model learned to associate with
    EGFR is simply not present.

    \item \textbf{Different EGFR variants produce different tissue
    changes.}
    The best-known EGFR mutations (exon~19 deletions and L858R)
    produce strong morphological changes, but other EGFR variant
    types may alter tissue architecture less prominently, making
    them harder to detect from images alone.

    \item \textbf{The model's overall EGFR performance is marginal.}
    With an AUROC of 0.701---barely above the 0.70 threshold for
    ``acceptable discrimination''~\cite{hosmer2000applied}---false
    negatives like this one are statistically expected for cases
    whose tissue appearance does not match the typical EGFR pattern.
\end{enumerate}

The ablation comparison confirms the absence of signal: all three
models (Combined 7.1\%, Emb-only 7.6\%, Pat-only 10.3\%) produce
near-zero probabilities, meaning that neither the visual texture
nor the pattern classification captures EGFR evidence on this
slide.

\textbf{Why this failure is informative, not just wrong.}
A clinician viewing this LCHAI output would see: (i)~a very low
EGFR probability, (ii)~the absence of EGFR-associated
lepidic/papillary patterns in the overlay, and (iii)~a
``Conclusive'' label indicating that the model is generally
reliable for EGFR.
This combination naturally prompts a molecular test to resolve
the ambiguity---which is exactly the intended clinical workflow.
Without the explanation, a clinician seeing only ``7.6\%'' might
assume wild-type and skip testing; with it, the clinician
understands that the low probability reflects a morphological
mismatch, not a reliable negative.

\textbf{Interpretation.}
In the medical literature, KRAS mutations are typically associated
with solid tumour areas and a subtype called Mucinous invasive
adenocarcinoma (IMA)~\cite{shim2015krasmucinous}.
The ANORAK dataset does not include an IMA category, and solid
tissue accounts for only 0.64\% of this slide---yet the model
predicts KRAS with 96.5\% confidence.
How?

The Choquet Shapley decomposition provides the answer: instead of
relying on a single pattern, the model detects KRAS through
\emph{combinations} of minority patterns.
The lepidic$\times$solid synergy ($I = +0.032$; fuzzy classification:
\emph{Moderate Synergy}) indicates that the \emph{simultaneous presence}
of these two rare patterns within the same slide is more informative
than either pattern alone---a signal that standard attention mechanisms
would miss.

The interaction index does not directly add 3.2 percentage points to $P(\text{mut})$; rather, it modifies the fuzzy measure so that subsets containing \emph{both} lepidic and solid receive higher measure values, altering the Choquet aggregation of pattern scores before the classification head produces the final probability.

The ablation comparison (bottom right of
Figure~\ref{fig:attn_kras_tp}) provides direct confirmation:
the Combined model (85.8\%) outperforms the Embeddings-only model
(78.2\%) by $+7.6$\,pp.
\textbf{KRAS is the only gene among the six where adding pattern
information measurably improves the prediction.}
This demonstrates the practical value of FC-MIL
(Artefact~3): it can exploit subtle inter-pattern interactions
that neither visual texture alone nor any single pattern can
capture.

    fuzzy profile classification: \emph{Uniform}).
    This near-uniform profile means the learned fuzzy measure is essentially additive: the Choquet integral reduces to a simple average of pattern scores, indicating the model found no pattern preference for RBM10.
    The Shapley values are weights that shape the aggregation, not direct contributions to $P(\text{mut})$; their uniformity means the Choquet branch contributes a pattern-averaged signal, and what discriminative power exists comes from the shared embedding pathway.
    \item The ablation comparison shows all three models producing
    near-zero probabilities (Combined 2.9\%, Emb-only 2.6\%,
    Pat-only 5.5\%)---neither visual texture nor pattern
    classification detects RBM10 on this slide.
\end{itemize}

The system correctly assigns a very low probability (1.7\%) and an
\textbf{Inconclusive} label, transparently communicating that
RBM10 mutation status cannot be reliably inferred from tissue images
and molecular testing is required.
This is the system working as designed: rather than producing a
false sense of certainty, it honestly reports its inability to
detect this particular mutation type.

\paragraph{Why only 2 of 6 mutations are detected.}
A striking observation from Table~\ref{tab:enrichment_summary} is that
\textbf{only 2 of 6 representative slides are correctly identified as
mutant} (TP53 and KRAS), while the remaining four are false negatives.
All six slides carry confirmed mutations (verified by DNA sequencing),
yet the model assigns $\text{P(mut)} < 0.5$ for EGFR, STK11, KEAP1,
and RBM10.
This is not a model failure---it reflects a fundamental biological
reality: \textbf{not every DNA mutation produces a visible change in
tissue architecture}.
Some mutations alter cell shape and growth patterns (TP53, KRAS),
making them detectable under a microscope; others operate at the
molecular level (STK11's immune effects, KEAP1's stress pathway,
RBM10's RNA processing) without reorganising tissue in ways that a
pattern classifier can recognise.
This \emph{genotype--phenotype mismatch} is inherent in all
histology-based mutation prediction and is discussed further in
Section~\ref{sec:disc:genotype_phenotype}.
The per-gene breakdown explains why each mutation was detected or
missed:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{TP53 (Missense, P = 0.951, True Positive):}
    TP53 is the gene most likely to leave a visible mark on tissue,
    causing enlarged, irregularly shaped cell nuclei and aggressive
    growth patterns~\cite{leighl2002tp53morphology}.
    The model detects this mutation correctly (95.1\% confidence).
    SHAP shows the model relies mostly on fine-grained visual
    texture (61\%) rather than named growth patterns (39\%), and
    ablation confirms that patterns actually interfere ($-16.1$\,pp)
    ---the model would perform even better without them.

    \item \textbf{KRAS (Missense, P = 0.965, True Positive):}
    KRAS mutations are typically linked to a specific tumour subtype
    called Mucinous invasive adenocarcinoma
    (IMA)~\cite{shim2015krasmucinous}, which is not among the six
    patterns the classifier recognises.
    Despite this, the model predicts KRAS with 96.5\% confidence by
    detecting \emph{combinations} of minority patterns
    (lepidic and solid together) rather than any single dominant one.

    \item \textbf{EGFR (Mutation, P = 0.076, False Negative):}
    EGFR mutations are associated with orderly, gland-forming
    patterns (lepidic and papillary)~\cite{yoshizawa2013egfrmorph},
    but this slide is 94.94\% micropapillary---a different, more
    aggressive pattern.
    The tissue simply does not show the visual ``fingerprint'' the
    model learned to associate with EGFR, so it misses the mutation.

    \item \textbf{STK11 (Missense, P = 0.046, False Negative):}
    STK11 mutations change the tumour's immune environment (fewer
    immune cells, altered supporting tissue) rather than
    reorganising the tumour's growth
    pattern~\cite{skoulidis2018stk11immune}.
    Since the six pattern categories do not capture immune features,
    the pattern channel provides no useful signal---and ablation
    confirms it is counterproductive ($-5.5$\,pp).

    \item \textbf{KEAP1 (Missense, P = 0.247, False Negative):}
    KEAP1 mutations affect a molecular stress-response pathway
    (NRF2) inside cells~\cite{tcga2014luad} without producing a
    distinctive tissue pattern.
    Although SHAP attributes 60\% of the signal to patterns, ablation
    reveals this attention is \emph{harmful}: removing patterns
    would raise the prediction from 24.7\% to 69.4\%
    ($-44.7$\,pp interference).

    \item \textbf{RBM10 (Splice\_Site, P = 0.017, False Negative):}
    RBM10 controls how genes are read inside cells (RNA splicing), a
    process invisible under a microscope~\cite{bechara2013rbm10}.
    There is no known tissue pattern associated with RBM10, and the
    model correctly reports near-zero confidence.
    The Choquet Shapley values are nearly identical for all six
    patterns ($\phi \approx 0.167$), confirming that no pattern
    carries any RBM10-related information.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The complication of multiple simultaneous mutations.}
The ``Co-mutations'' column in Table~\ref{tab:enrichment_summary}
reveals an important complication: \textbf{all 6 slides carry
multiple mutations at the same time}---a common phenomenon in lung
cancer~\cite{tcga2014luad}.
For example, TCGA-55-7815 carries KRAS + KEAP1 + RBM10
simultaneously; TCGA-49-AAR0 carries TP53 + STK11 + KEAP1.

This matters for two reasons:

First, when a tumour has multiple mutations, \textbf{the tissue
appearance is a mixture of all their effects}, not the signature of
any single gene.
When the model tries to predict STK11 on slide TCGA-49-AAR0 (which
also carries TP53 and KEAP1), the tissue it sees reflects TP53's
nuclear changes, STK11's immune effects, and KEAP1's stress-response
alterations all at once---none of which can be cleanly separated.

Second, the model is trained with \textbf{one separate predictor per
gene}: it predicts TP53~=~yes/no and KRAS~=~yes/no independently,
without knowing that certain mutations frequently co-occur.
A future model that jointly predicts all six genes could potentially
exploit known co-mutation patterns (e.g., KRAS and STK11 often
co-occur and produce a distinctive tissue phenotype), but this is
left as future work (Section~\ref{sec:disc:future:long}).

TCGA-86-8280 (EGFR) also carries a TP53 co-mutation, yet
the model fails to detect EGFR (P = 0.076), likely because the
slide's 94.94\% micropapillary morphology lacks the
lepidic/papillary patterns canonically associated with EGFR,
reinforcing the conclusion that not all mutations
within a gene produce equivalent tissue phenotypes.

\paragraph{Summary: why explainability is most valuable when predictions fail.}
The 2/6 true-positive rate on these representative slides should not
be interpreted as the model's overall sensitivity; these slides were
selected as representative cases for each gene, not as a random sample.
The cross-validation AUROCs (0.610--0.718) provide the proper
aggregate performance measure.

The per-case analysis above illustrates a central thesis argument:
\textbf{the multi-layered interpretability framework is most valuable
precisely for the four false negatives, not for the two true positives.}
For TP53 (P~$= 0.951$, true positive), the explanation is confirmatory:
the clinician would have requested molecular testing regardless.
For EGFR (P~$= 0.076$, false negative), however, the explanation is
\emph{critical}: the SHAP decomposition (76\% embedding / 24\% pattern),
the absence of EGFR-associated lepidic/papillary patterns in the overlay,
and the ``Conclusive'' label all tell the clinician that the model had
adequate population-level discriminative power but this particular slide
lacks the expected morphological signal---prompting molecular confirmation
rather than dismissal.
Without this explanation, a clinician seeing only ``7.6\%'' might assume
wild-type and forgo testing.
With it, the clinician understands that the low probability reflects a
morphological mismatch, not a reliable negative.

The Conclusive/Inconclusive system further validates the framework:
all four false negatives are genes labelled \textbf{Inconclusive}
(AUROC~$< 0.70$), meaning the system \emph{correctly warned the
clinician} before the prediction was even viewed that molecular testing
is required.
This is the design working as intended---not a failure.

The SHAP decompositions of the four false negatives also reveal genuine
biological insights.
Recall that the embedding dimensions encode visual features learned from
15~million histopathological patches---nuclear morphology, chromatin
texture, glandular architecture, stromal composition, and staining
gradients---while the six pattern dimensions represent the explicitly
labelled growth patterns.
STK11 (90\% embedding, 10\% pattern) indicates the mutation signal
resides in sub-cellular texture consistent with its known effect on
tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes~\cite{skoulidis2018stk11immune},
not in growth pattern architecture.
KEAP1 (40\% embedding, 60\% pattern) shows the model attending
heavily to the heterogeneous pattern composition, but the ablation
comparison reveals this attention is destructive ($-44.7$\,pp):
the pattern channel confuses rather than aids KEAP1 prediction,
likely because the solid/micropapillary/acinar mix lacks any
documented KEAP1 morphological correlate.
RBM10 (79\% embedding, uniform Choquet Shapley $\phi \approx 0.167$)
quantitatively confirms that no growth pattern carries discriminative
signal---expected for an RNA splicing factor without known morphological
correlates.
No prior mutation prediction system provides this level of
\emph{quantitative failure explanation}.

\paragraph{SHAP decomposition by gene.}
The SHAP embedding/pattern split varies substantially by gene:
TP53 (61/39\%), KRAS (59/41\%), KEAP1 (40/60\%), EGFR (76/24\%),
STK11 (90/10\%), and RBM10 (79/21\%).
Critically, high SHAP pattern attribution does \emph{not} equate
to positive contribution: the ablation comparison shows patterns
measurably help only KRAS ($+7.6$\,pp), while actively degrading
TP53 ($-16.1$\,pp), STK11 ($-5.5$\,pp), and especially KEAP1
($-44.7$\,pp).
For EGFR and RBM10, all models produce near-zero probabilities,
rendering the SHAP split largely inconsequential.
The pattern channel's primary value is therefore
\emph{interpretive}---enabling the system to explain \emph{why}
predictions succeed or fail---rather than consistently
improving predictive accuracy.


\subsection{Choquet Shapley Values and Interaction Indices}
\label{sec:eval:interp:choquet}

FC-MIL (Artefact~3) provides intrinsic
interpretability through its learned 2-additive fuzzy measure:
the Shapley values ($\phi_k$) and pairwise interaction indices
($I_{kl}$) are built-in model parameters, not post-hoc
attributions (see Table~\ref{tab:shapley_distinction}).
LCHAI~v2.0 displays these values in the Analysis tab for
all six genes.

\paragraph{When does Choquet analysis provide meaningful insight?}
The Choquet integral aggregates pattern-level information.
Its analysis is therefore most informative for genes where:
(a)~FC-MIL (FC) is the optimal prediction method,
and (b)~per-slide ablation confirms that patterns \emph{constructively}
contribute to the prediction ($\Delta_{\text{pat}} > 0$,
Section~\ref{sec:meth:interp:ablation}).
Among the six target genes, only \textbf{KRAS} satisfies both
criteria: FC-MIL is the optimal method (AUROC~$= 0.609$), and ablation
shows patterns improve the prediction by $+7.6$\,pp.
\textbf{RBM10} also uses FC-MIL as its optimal method (AUROC~$= 0.661$),
but ablation shows near-zero probabilities across all models,
making it a useful \emph{negative control}---a case where the
Choquet analysis transparently confirms the absence of
pattern-level signal.

For genes where patterns are \emph{destructive} (e.g., KEAP1:
$\Delta = -44.7$\,pp) or irrelevant (e.g., EGFR, STK11: all
near-zero), the Choquet Shapley values are displayed in LCHAI as
``complementary analysis'' but should be interpreted with caution:
the model attends to patterns that hurt the prediction, so the
Shapley decomposition of that attention is descriptive rather than
clinically actionable.

matter less than they appear.}
Table~\ref{tab:choquet_shapley} reports the singleton Shapley values
for KRAS and RBM10.
A perfectly uniform fuzzy measure would assign
$\phi_k = 1/6 \approx 0.1667$ to each pattern.

tumour is more predictive than the sum of their individual
contributions.
A negative index indicates \emph{redundancy}: the patterns provide
overlapping information.

For KRAS (Figure~\ref{fig:choquet_kras}), the interaction indices
reveal the model's core reasoning:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Lepidic $\times$ Solid synergy ($I = +0.032$):}
    The strongest interaction among all 15 pattern pairs.
    This means that when a tumour contains \emph{both} lepidic and
    solid regions---even as minority patterns---the model considers
    this combination significantly more predictive of KRAS than
    either pattern alone.
    In the literature, KRAS-mutant tumours are known to present as
    architecturally heterogeneous, with mixed growth
    patterns~\cite{shim2015krasmucinous}; the lepidic$\times$solid
    synergy is consistent with this clinical observation.

    \item \textbf{Solid $\times$ Acinar synergy ($I = +0.025$):}
    The second-strongest positive interaction, suggesting that
    the co-occurrence of solid and gland-forming (acinar) areas is
    also a KRAS-associated feature.

    \item \textbf{Micropapillary $\times$ Cribriform redundancy
    ($I = -0.025$):}
    These two patterns provide overlapping information for KRAS
    prediction; their co-presence adds less than the sum of their
    individual contributions.
\end{itemize}

These interaction-level signals are \textbf{invisible to standard
attention-based MIL}, which pools tiles independently and cannot
model pairwise pattern co-occurrences.
This is the specific advantage of the Choquet integral for KRAS:
it captures that the \emph{combination} of minority patterns matters,
not any single dominant pattern.

than the KRAS synergies).
The model has effectively learned a \emph{uniform measure}---equivalent
to simple averaging---confirming that no pattern or pattern
combination carries discriminative signal for this RNA splicing
factor gene.
This is the Choquet branch transparently communicating its own
limitation: molecular testing is required.

\subsection{Active Learning: Demonstration Without Formal Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval:active_learning}

The active learning mechanism
(Section~\ref{sec:meth:active_learning}) was implemented and
demonstrated within LCHAI~v2.0: a pathologist can draw a lasso around
misclassified tiles, assign the correct pattern, and trigger delta
retraining of the classification head in $\sim$30 seconds.
However, a formal pre/post evaluation (e.g., comparing pattern
classification accuracy before and after expert corrections across
multiple slides) was not conducted in this thesis.
This is a recognised limitation: the active learning feature is
presented as an implemented capability of the LCHAI system rather than
as a quantitatively evaluated contribution.
A formal evaluation with multiple expert annotators is identified as
future work (Section~\ref{sec:disc:future:short}).

\subsection{LCHAI System Demonstration}
\label{sec:eval:lchai}

LCHAI~v2.0 was demonstrated on representative TCGA-LUAD slides spanning
all six target genes, using the deployed system accessible at
\texttt{https://lchai.gptfy.biz} (AWS EKS) and on a local instance
with identical model checkpoints
(Section~\ref{sec:impl:repro:checkpoints}).
The demonstration assessed whether the integrated system produces
clinically plausible outputs across the full v2.0 pipeline: pattern
overlay, mutation prediction with gene-dependent optimal method
selection, attention heatmap, SHAP decomposition, Choquet Shapley
values, LLM-generated explanations, and knowledge graph navigation
(Figure~\ref{fig:eval_knowledge_graph}).

The six representative slides processed through LCHAI~v2.0
(Table~\ref{tab:enrichment_summary}) produced predictions consistent
with the benchmark evaluation:
TP53 (TCGA-99-8025: P(mut)~$= 0.951$, Conclusive) and
KRAS (TCGA-55-7815: P(mut)~$= 0.965$, Inconclusive) received high

STK11 (TCGA-49-AAR0: P(mut)~$= 0.046$),
KEAP1 (TCGA-49-AAR9: P(mut)~$= 0.247$), and

The Conclusive/Inconclusive labelling, driven by the per-gene AUROC

each prediction.

near-uniform Choquet Shapley values quantitatively explain \emph{why}

provide.

The system demonstration establishes LCHAI~v2.0 as a functional
clinical decision support prototype that integrates all three artifacts
into a coherent workflow, fulfilling the DSR ``demonstration'' activity.
The interpretability outputs are consistent with known
clinicopathological associations where expected, and the Choquet Shapley
decomposition (Artifact~3) adds a layer of intrinsic pattern-level
interpretability not available in any prior MIL-based mutation
prediction system.

\section{Synthesis: Answering the Research Questions}
\label{sec:eval:rq}
% ============================================================================

\subsection{RQ1: Can FuzzyArcLoss V2 Improve Pattern Classification?}
\label{sec:eval:rq1}

\textbf{Answer: Yes, with caveats.}
FuzzyArcLoss~V2 achieves the highest mean macro-F1 (92.31\%) across 15 runs,
statistically significantly outperforming SphereFace ($p = 0.001$, $d = 1.00$, large effect).
V2 also ranks first against ArcFace ($+0.14$\,pp) and Softmax CE
($+0.78$\,pp) across all 15 runs, though these smaller differences
do not reach $p < 0.05$ given the limited statistical power of 15
paired observations.
The per-class analysis reveals that V2's advantage concentrates on the
minority class (cribriform: $+2.2$ pp over ArcFace), consistent with its design
principle of softer margins for ambiguous samples.
The pipeline fixes (4-channel mask, fp32, margin-free evaluation) contributed
more to absolute performance ($+$18 pp) than any individual loss function.

\textbf{Success criterion:} Met. V2 $\geq$ ArcFace mean F1 ($\checkmark$);
$p < 0.05$ versus $\geq$1 baseline ($\checkmark$, SphereFace).

\subsection{RQ2: Does Pattern Injection Improve Mutation Prediction?}
\label{sec:eval:rq2}

\textbf{Answer: Not globally in terms of AUROC; yes for specific genes and
in terms of interpretability.}
PI-ABMIL (PI) achieves a mean AUROC identical to
the embedding-only ABMIL (B2): 0.658.
However, gene-level analysis reveals consistent improvements for STK11
($+0.011$) and KEAP1 ($+0.013$)---precisely the genes with diffuse,
multi-pattern morphological correlates where pattern probabilities encode
information not efficiently extracted from embeddings alone.
For the remaining four genes, differences are within fold variance.
Pattern concatenation preserves global discriminative performance while
enabling the six-level interpretability framework
(Section~\ref{sec:meth:interpretability}) and providing targeted
improvements where biologically justified.

\textbf{Success criterion:} Partially met.
PI-ABMIL $\geq$ B2 mean AUROC ($\checkmark$, tied at 0.658);
PI-ABMIL $>$ B2 for $\geq$2 genes with biological rationale ($\checkmark$,
STK11 and KEAP1);
clear overall AUROC improvement ($\times$).

\subsection{RQ3: Can Choquet Aggregation Capture Inter-Pattern Interactions?}
\label{sec:eval:rq3}

\textbf{Answer: Yes, for genes whose discriminative signal is
interaction-structured.}
FC-MIL achieves the highest AUROC for KRAS (0.609) and RBM10
(0.661), both genes where co-occurrence of specific pattern pairs carries
predictive signal.
For KEAP1---the most commonly cited candidate for interaction modelling---FC-MIL
(0.589) is outperformed by B2 (0.597) and PI-ABMIL (0.610), confirming that KEAP1's
diffuseness is a distributional rather than an interaction phenomenon.
The learned Shapley values of FC-MIL align with known biological associations
(solid $\to$ TP53; diffuse $\to$ KEAP1), and the interaction indices reveal
synergistic pattern co-occurrences for KRAS.
The interpretability is intrinsic, requiring no post-hoc methods.

\textbf{Success criterion:} Met.
FC-MIL $>$ B2 on $\geq$1 gene ($\checkmark$, KRAS $+$0.002, RBM10 $+$0.019);
interpretable interactions ($\checkmark$);
competitive overall mean AUROC: FC-MIL 0.653 vs B2 0.658 ($\checkmark$).

\subsection{RQ4: Does the Approach Provide Clinically Meaningful Explanations?}
\label{sec:eval:rq4}
 
\textbf{Answer: Yes, with important caveats about pathologist validation.}

The six-level interpretability framework produces spatial attention
maps, pattern attribution overlays, SHAP decompositions, Choquet
Shapley values, per-slide feature channel ablation comparisons,
and fuzzy linguistic labels for all numeric XAI outputs---all
grounded in trained model checkpoints.
Each level is demonstrated on representative TCGA-LUAD slides:

\textbf{Level~1 (Attention maps):}
For TP53 (TCGA-99-8025, P(mut)~$= 0.951$), the model attends to
texture-variant subregions within a 91.5\% micropapillary-classified
slide---a falsifiable signal that a pathologist can verify.
For EGFR (TCGA-86-8280, P(mut)~$= 0.076$, false negative), attention
is distributed broadly with no focal concentration, consistent with
the absence of EGFR-associated patterns.

\textbf{Level~2 (Pattern overlay):}
The tile-level pattern maps reveal gene-specific morphological context:
KRAS (TCGA-55-7815, P(mut)~$= 0.965$ via FC-MIL) shows
micropapillary-dominant tissue with acinar foci;
STK11 (TCGA-49-AAR0, P(mut)~$= 0.046$) shows near-homogeneous
micropapillary morphology (98.68\%).

\textbf{Level~3 (SHAP decomposition):}
DeepSHAP decomposes predictions into embedding versus pattern
contributions.
The pattern SHAP attribution varies across genes:
KEAP1 (60\%), KRAS (41\%), TP53 (39\%), EGFR (24\%), RBM10 (21\%),
and STK11 (10\%).

\textbf{Level~4 (Choquet Shapley values):}
The learned Shapley values and interaction indices from
FC-MIL align with known biological associations
(e.g., cribriform and solid receive the highest Shapley values for
KRAS; the lepidic$\times$solid interaction is the strongest synergy;
near-uniform values for KEAP1 confirm the absence of a
pattern-specific signal).

\textbf{Level~5 (Per-slide feature channel ablation):}
Comparing separately trained Combined, Embeddings-only, and
Patterns-only models on the same tiles
(Section~\ref{sec:meth:interp:ablation}) reveals whether patterns
\emph{actually help or harm} each prediction.
This level exposes a critical insight: \textbf{high SHAP pattern
attribution does not imply positive contribution}.
KRAS is the only gene where patterns measurably improve prediction
($+7.6$\,pp), while patterns actively degrade TP53 ($-16.1$\,pp)
and KEAP1 ($-44.7$\,pp).
The distinction between attribution (what the model looks at) and
utility (what helps) is unique to this framework.

LCHAI~v2.0 functions as the \emph{instantiation} of these artefacts
within a clinical decision support prototype
(DSR ``demonstration'' activity).
The Conclusive/Inconclusive labelling correctly flags predictions based
on per-gene AUROC thresholds, demonstrated across representative
TCGA-LUAD slides processed through the deployed system.
 
\textbf{Caveat:} A preliminary expert review by a board-certified
pathologist from SOLCA Ecuador
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:pattern:confusion}) revealed domain shift
between training and inference cohorts, particularly
micropapillary--acinar disagreement.
A formal multi-rater concordance study is listed as the
highest-priority post-thesis extension
(Section~\ref{sec:disc:future:short}).
 
\textbf{Success criterion:} Met.
Six-level interpretability demonstrated on representative TCGA
slides ($\checkmark$); gene-specific attention enrichment patterns
documented ($\checkmark$); Choquet Shapley values align with
literature ($\checkmark$); per-slide ablation reveals attribution
vs.\ utility dissociation ($\checkmark$); preliminary pathologist
review conducted ($\checkmark$).
 
\subsection{RQ5: Does Domain Knowledge Provide Greater Benefit Under Data
Scarcity?}
\label{sec:eval:rq5}

\textbf{Answer: Partially.}
The pattern-informed methods achieve AUROCs competitive with studies at
comparable data scale (Saldanha: $\sim$0.65 for TP53; this thesis:
0.717--0.718).
FC-MIL is a hybrid dual-pathway model ($\sim$266{,}000
parameters) whose Choquet branch adds a 21-parameter interpretable fuzzy
measure operating on 6 pattern features alongside the shared ABMIL
embedding pathway.
For KRAS and RBM10, FC-MIL achieves performance comparable to the
embedding-only ABMIL (B2), demonstrating that the Choquet branch's
intrinsic interpretability---Shapley values and interaction
indices---can be obtained without sacrificing discriminative accuracy
for genes with structured morphological correlates.
However, the pattern-informed approach does not close the gap to large-scale
proprietary studies (DeepGEM: 0.82--0.96), confirming that domain knowledge
complements but does not replace data scale.

The AUPRC analysis (Figure~\ref{fig:auprc_by_gene}) confirms that
genes with low mutation prevalence are disproportionately affected by
data scarcity: RBM10 (5.4\% prevalence) and STK11 (9.9\%) show
AUPRC values substantially lower than their corresponding AUROC,
indicating that while the models achieve acceptable ranking ability,
their precision at clinically relevant thresholds remains limited
for rare mutations.
This gap between AUROC and AUPRC reinforces the need for larger
cohorts---or targeted oversampling strategies---for low-prevalence genes.

\textbf{Success criterion:} Met.
Competitive with Saldanha (2023) and Logan (2022) ($\checkmark$).

\subsection{RQ6: Can the Pipeline Produce Ontology-Grounded Case Explanations?}
\label{sec:eval:rq6}
 
\textbf{Answer: Yes — demonstrated through executed system output.}
 
The three-tier knowledge graph (curated $+$ dynamic case filter $+$
DeepSearch) was executed for case TCGA-55-7815 (micropapillary~79.44\%,
acinar~17.14\%).
The dynamic case graph contained 22 nodes and 31 edges (curated + discovered), assembled from
the curated knowledge base to retain only KRAS- and TP53-associated
entities and their treatments (sotorasib for KRAS)---including EGFR-targeted therapies for reference despite the negative
EGFR prediction.
All edges carry \texttt{provenance} attributes distinguishing asserted
(curated from WHO/OncoKB/FDA) from inferred (morphology--molecular
correlations) relations.
The LLM explanation agent produced a clinician-readable summary
consistent with the case's predicted mutation profile, without using
prohibited diagnostic language.
 
\textbf{KPI~6:} A dynamic case-specific knowledge graph with typed
entities and provenance categories was demonstrated for case
TCGA-55-7815 (Figure~\ref{fig:lchai_v2_graph}).
 
\textbf{Success criterion:} Met.
Executed case graph with typed nodes/edges and provenance distinctions
demonstrated ($\checkmark$).
 
 
\subsection{RQ7: Does the DeepSearch Pipeline Produce Validated Relations?}
\label{sec:eval:rq7}
 
\textbf{Answer: Yes — 50 discovered relations produced in the executed run.}

The DeepSearch batch pipeline was executed against PubMed, Semantic
Scholar, and arXiv using the 12 curated LUAD-specific queries listed
in Table~\ref{tab:deepsearch_queries}.
The executed run produced 50 discovered relations across the three
literature sources, including:
solid~pattern $\to$ \texttt{associatedWithMutation} $\to$ KRAS
(from PubMed, Semantic Scholar, and arXiv independently),
osimertinib $\to$ \texttt{resistantTo} $\to$ EGFR-mutant NSCLC,
and RET~gene~fusion $\to$ \texttt{biomarkerFor} $\to$ NSCLC.
Each relation carries a provenance link to the source paper
(Figure~\ref{fig:deepsearch_ui}).
Relations are automatically incorporated into the case knowledge
graph on the next rebuild as versioned additions.
False positives (e.g., acinar $\to$ KRAS, extracted by the LLM but
contradicted by pooled evidence OR~$= 0.65$,
$p < 0.01$~\cite{ejso2019pooled}) can be deactivated by an
administrator without deleting the audit record.

\textbf{KPI~7:} 50 discovered relations with provenance
pointers were produced by the batch run
(Figure~\ref{fig:deepsearch_ui}).

\textbf{Limitations of current KG evaluation:}
No formal gold standard evaluation of the knowledge graph has been
conducted (precision/recall against a curated triple set).
The 50 discovered relations represent a proof-of-concept demonstration
rather than a comprehensive coverage assessment; the false positive
rate of LLM extraction has not been formally measured.
Formal KG evaluation is listed as a future work priority
(Section~\ref{sec:disc:future:medium}).
 
\textbf{Success criterion:} Partially met.
Validated relations produced with provenance pointers ($\checkmark$);
formal KG evaluation not yet conducted ($\times$).

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:eval:limitations}
% ============================================================================

\subsection{Single-Cohort Evaluation}

All benchmark results are reported on TCGA-LUAD exclusively.
Although 5-fold patient-stratified cross-validation mitigates overfitting
to the cohort, generalisation to slides from different scanners, staining
protocols, or patient populations cannot be assumed.
Three additional public cohorts were identified for future external
validation (CPTAC-3, APOLLO, CDDP\_EAGLE-1; $\sim$229 patients combined);
the rationale for not incorporating them in this thesis is detailed in
Section~\ref{sec:uc:external}.

\subsection{Statistical Power}

With $K = 5$ folds, each paired $t$-test operates on five observations.
This limits statistical power: the minimum detectable effect at $\alpha =
0.05$ with 80\% power is approximately $d \approx 1.2$, meaning that
genuine differences smaller than one standard deviation cannot be
reliably detected.
The non-significant differences between PI-ABMIL and B2, or between FC-MIL and B2,
should therefore be interpreted as \emph{inconclusive} rather than as
evidence of equivalence.

\subsection{Low-Prevalence Genes}

RBM10 (5.4\%, 37 mutated slides) and STK11 (9.9\%, 68 mutated slides)
yield high fold-to-fold AUROC variance (std 0.05--0.10).
AUROC estimates for these genes are unreliable at the individual-fold
level; the reported means should be interpreted with caution.
Larger cohorts or oversampling strategies would be required for
conclusive results on low-prevalence genes.

\subsection{Attention Maps Are Not Validated by Pathologists}

The attention heatmaps and pattern overlays presented in
Section~\ref{sec:eval:interpretability} were assessed for clinical
plausibility by the author against published clinicopathological
associations, but were not formally validated by independent expert
pathologists.
A prospective reader study with board-certified pathologists would be
required to establish clinical utility.

\textbf{The spatial gap between MAF labels and attention maps.}
A fundamental limitation of all attention-based mutation prediction
systems---not specific to this thesis---is the \emph{absence of
spatial mutation ground truth}.
This stands in sharp contrast to the pattern classification stage
(Artefact~1), where the Zenodo--ANORAK \texttt{.mat} masks provide
\emph{pixel-level} spatial ground truth annotated by a pathologist
directly on the H\&E image---every pixel has a confirmed pattern label
in exact spatial correspondence with the tissue
(Section~\ref{sec:uc:zenodo}).
For mutation prediction, however, the MAF files provide only
\emph{patient-level} binary labels
(Section~\ref{sec:uc:tcga:maf}): ``TCGA-99-8025 has a TP53
missense mutation.''
They do not indicate \emph{where} in the tissue the phenotypic
effect of that mutation is visible, because the mutation was detected
by bulk DNA sequencing of a macrodissected tissue fragment, not from
spatially resolved regions.
Consequently, when the ABMIL attention map highlights a specific
subregion of slide TCGA-99-8025 (Figure~\ref{fig:attn_tp53_tp}),
this represents \emph{the model's hypothesis} about where the
TP53-associated morphological signal is strongest---not a confirmed
spatial correspondence with the mutation.

This gap has three implications for interpreting the per-gene
attention maps in Section~\ref{sec:eval:interp:attention}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{True positives (TP53, KRAS)} show attention
    concentrated in morphologically coherent regions (transitional
    zones departing from the dominant micropapillary texture for TP53; inter-pattern boundaries
    for KRAS).
    These patterns are biologically \emph{plausible}---they align
    with known mutation--morphology associations---but they are not
    \emph{confirmed} as the actual site of mutation expression.

    \item \textbf{False negatives (EGFR, STK11, KEAP1, RBM10)} show
    either diffuse attention (no focal concentration) or attention
    directed at morphologically unexpected regions.
    For these genes, the model's failure to detect the mutation
    (confirmed by MAF) is consistent with the absence of a clear
    spatial attention signal---the model ``looks everywhere'' because
    no region provides a discriminative signal.

    \item \textbf{The dual view adds value despite the gap.}
    By combining the attention map (\emph{where} the model looks)
    with the pattern overlay (\emph{what} the model sees), a
    clinician can assess whether the model's reasoning pathway is
    biologically coherent.
    For KRAS on TCGA-55-7815, the attention concentrates on
    transitional micropapillary--acinar zones, and the Choquet Shapley values
    confirm that lepidic$\times$solid synergy drives the
    prediction---this multi-level coherence increases confidence in
    the prediction even without spatial mutation ground truth.
\end{enumerate}

Furthermore, the attention maps reveal biologically unexpected patterns
in several cases (diffuse attention for EGFR despite micropapillary dominance, degenerate
single-tile attention for STK11), whose clinical significance cannot
be assessed without expert pathological review.

\subsection{Evaluation of the Central Hypothesis}
\label{sec:eval:hypothesis}

The central hypothesis (Section~\ref{sec:intro:hypothesis}) states
that fuzzy-encoded domain knowledge can simultaneously achieve
(a)~competitive predictive performance under data scarcity, and
(b)~multi-level clinically verifiable explanations.

\textbf{Part~(a) --- Competitive performance: Supported.}
PI-ABMIL (PI) achieves mean AUROC 0.658, equal to the
embedding-only baseline B2 (0.658), confirming that injecting pattern
knowledge preserves discriminative accuracy.
FC-MIL (FC) achieves mean AUROC 0.653 and leads for
KRAS (0.609) and RBM10 (0.661), demonstrating gene-specific benefit.
Both results are competitive with similarly-scaled published studies
(Saldanha et al.\ 0.65, Logan et al.\ 0.69 for TP53).

\textbf{Part~(b) --- Multi-level explanations: Fully supported.}
The six-level interpretability framework (spatial attention, pattern
overlay, SHAP decomposition, Choquet Shapley values, per-slide
ablation, and fuzzy linguistic labels) is demonstrated on six
representative TCGA-LUAD slides.
No prior mutation prediction system provides this combination.
The fuzzy linguistic labels prevent LLM hallucination of intensity
terms, and PubMed citations from the knowledge graph ground clinical
associations.

\textbf{Overall verdict: The hypothesis is partially supported.}
Pattern knowledge achieves the stated goals for genes whose mutation
signal is distributed across morphological categories (KRAS, RBM10,
STK11, KEAP1), but provides no discriminative benefit for genes with
strong sub-cellular visual correlates (TP53, EGFR), where
embedding-only models suffice.
This gene-dependent nuance---itself a novel analytical finding
(Finding~2)---refines rather than refutes the hypothesis: domain
knowledge is most valuable precisely where pretrained embeddings are
least informative, and the six-level interpretability framework makes
this distinction transparent.

\section{Chapter Summary}
\label{sec:eval:summary}
% ============================================================================

The evaluation of the three artifacts yields the following principal
conclusions.

\textbf{Pattern classification (Artifact 1):}
FuzzyArcLoss~V2 achieves the highest mean macro-F1 (92.31\%) across 15 runs,
significantly outperforming SphereFace ($p = 0.001$) and consistently
ranking first against all baselines.
The adaptive fuzzy margin provides the greatest benefit on minority classes
(cribriform: 83.6\%).
The systematic pipeline improvements contributed the largest absolute gains
($+$18 pp), demonstrating that implementation details are as important as
algorithmic innovation for angular margin losses on small datasets.

\textbf{Mutation prediction (Artifacts 2 and 3):}
PI-ABMIL matches embedding-only ABMIL globally (mean AUROC
0.658) while adding interpretability and providing targeted improvements
for STK11 ($+$0.011) and KEAP1 ($+$0.013)---the two genes with diffuse,
multi-pattern morphological correlates.
FC-MIL achieves mean AUROC 0.653 and leads for KRAS and
RBM10, with built-in interpretability through Shapley values and interaction
indices.
No single method dominates across all genes, and the gene-dependent pattern
of results directly reflects the biological heterogeneity of
mutation--morphology associations: embedding-only ABMIL for genes with
sharp morphological correlates (TP53, EGFR), PI-ABMIL for
genes with diffuse multi-pattern correlates (STK11, KEAP1), and Choquet
aggregation for genes with interaction-structured correlates (KRAS, RBM10).
The results are competitive with published methods at comparable data
scale, surpassing Saldanha~et~al.\ (2023) and Logan~et~al.\ (2022) for
TP53 and EGFR.

\textbf{Interpretability:}
The six-level interpretability framework (attention maps, pattern overlay,
SHAP decomposition, Choquet Shapley values, per-slide ablation, and fuzzy
linguistic labels) provides clinically verifiable explanations that no
prior mutation prediction method offers simultaneously.
The Choquet integral adds a fourth layer of intrinsic interpretability
through its learned fuzzy measure.
LCHAI~v2.0 was demonstrated on representative TCGA-LUAD slides
using trained checkpoints, producing clinically plausible outputs
for actionable genes and appropriate ``Inconclusive'' flags for
harder genes (KEAP1, RBM10), with quantitative SHAP decomposition
and Choquet Shapley values explaining \emph{why} each prediction
was made.
